# Sheltering: shelters, basements and staying indoors {#ukrytie}

Sheltering protects you from a danger that comes from outside and is relatively short-lived: radioactive fallout, a toxic gas leak, an air attack, a windstorm, shooting. A shelter does not have to be a bunker – in most cases it is the **cellar, basement or an inner room** of the building you are in. What matters is knowing where to go, what to take and how to behave.

::: urgent
**Act now – you must take shelter**

- Go to the **nearest solid building** – do not run a long way across open ground.
- During an **air-raid alert**, go to a shelter, cellar or underground car park; if there is none, to an inner room with **at least two walls** between you and the outside, away from windows.
- During a **chemical or radiation release**, close windows and doors, turn off ventilation, seal gaps and turn on the radio.
- Take your **shelter bag**: documents, medicines, water, food, light, radio, blanket.
- Help your neighbours and follow the instructions of the authorities and the shelter warden.
- Go outside only after the **All clear** signal or when instructed.
:::

## When to shelter and when not {#ukrytie-kedy}

| Situation | Where |
|---|---|
| Air attack, missile attack | Shelter, cellar, underground car park, metro station; otherwise an inner room (two-wall rule) |
| Radiation accident | Cellar or the middle of the building, as far as possible from the roof and outer walls; seal the room |
| Release of a dangerous chemical | A closed room, windows and ventilation shut; with heavy gases (chlorine) an upper floor, not the cellar |
| Windstorm, hail, tornado | The lowest floor, an inner room without windows |
| Shooter, attacker | A locked room with the door barricaded ([Terrorism](#terorizmus)) |
| **Flood** | **Not the cellar!** Go to upper floors or evacuate |
| **Fire** | **Not sheltering but leaving** – shelter only if there is no way out |

## Types of shelter – example: Slovakia {#ukrytie-druhy}

Shelter systems differ from country to country (see [Shelters in Europe](#kryty-europa)). As an example, Slovak legislation (Act No. 42/1994 on civil protection and Decree No. 532/2006) distinguishes these protective structures:

- **Blast-resistant shelters** – protect against the blast wave, radiation, chemical and biological agents, and have filtered ventilation. Protection factor at least **100**.
- **Gas-tight shelters** – gas-tight, protect against radiation and dangerous substances. Protection factor at least **100**.
- **Simple self-built shelters (JÚBS)** – basements, cellars and other suitable spaces that are adapted only when a threat arises, according to the municipal shelter plan. Protection factor at least **50**. They make up the vast majority of protective structures.

The **protection factor** tells you how many times weaker the radiation is in the shelter than outside. With a factor of 50 you receive only 2 % of the dose you would get in the open.

According to the Slovak interior ministry, Slovakia has about **1,530 blast-resistant and gas-tight shelters** for about 4 % of the population. Everyone else would shelter in simple shelters, which must be ready to receive people **within 12 hours** and have improved protection **within 24 hours**. Blast-resistant and gas-tight shelters not used in peacetime must be ready within 2 hours; those in other use (for example as storage) within 12 hours.

## How to find out where your shelter is {#ukrytie-kde}

1. **Town hall** – municipalities usually have a **shelter plan** listing the shelters and who should go where. Ask to see it.
2. **Regional or district civil-protection office** keeps a register of shelters.
3. **The building manager** or your employer knows whether the building has a shelter or a designated basement.
4. **Signs** – shelters are usually marked with a sign (e.g. "ÚKRYT", "Schutzraum", "Schron") and the international civil-defence emblem (a blue triangle on an orange background).
5. **The map in the app** shows shelters and other places from open data (OpenStreetMap), links to official maps across Europe, and lets you add your own shelter.

::: tip
Once you know where your shelter is, **walk the route to it** – at night, with an older person, with a pushchair. Write it into your family plan and onto your wallet card.
:::

## Prepare an improvised shelter at home {#ukrytie-domov}

If there is no blast-resistant shelter nearby, prepare a **cellar or inner room** in advance, following civil-protection guidance for simple shelters.

### Choosing the room {#ukrytie-vyber}

- Preferably **below ground** – the deeper, the better the protection.
- **Few windows and doors**, not in a corner of the building, ideally surrounded by other rooms.
- Close to the **toilet and bathroom**.
- Allow **1–1.5 m² of floor space per person** and a ceiling height of at least 2.1 m.
- In an apartment building without a cellar: **a hallway or bathroom in the middle of the flat**, on a middle floor (not the top floor and not a ground floor with large windows).

### Reinforcing and sealing {#ukrytie-utesnenie}

- Cover **windows and vents at ground level** with boards or sheet metal and bank them up with soil, sand, sandbags or bricks. The thickness should match the outer wall, and the material should **overlap the opening by 30 cm**.
- If the entrance leads straight outside, build a **blast wall** in front of the door (at least 50 cm thick and 1.7 m high) that does not block the entrance.
- **Seal all gaps** around windows, doors, pipe penetrations and unused vents: expanding foam, duct tape, self-adhesive foam strip, plastic sheeting, plaster, putty, clay.
- For natural ventilation use **two ducts** on opposite walls – the intake draws air 1.5–2 m above the ground and opens 0.5 m above the floor; the exhaust opens under the ceiling and ends at least 1 m higher. Allow about 10 cm² of cross-section per person (for 10 people a pipe about 11–12 cm in diameter). The ducts must be closable.

**Indicative material thickness for protection factor 50 / 100 (cm):**

| Material | PF 50 | PF 100 |
|---|---|---|
| Solid bricks | 45 | 50 |
| Dressed stone | 27 | 33 |
| Rubble stone | 35 | 40 |
| Plain concrete | 33 | 40 |
| Reinforced concrete | 28 | 34 |
| Clay (soil) | 35 | 45 |
| Dry sand | 50 | 60 |
| Wood | 90 | 110 |

### Equipping the shelter {#ukrytie-vybavenie}

- **Water: 6 litres per person per day** (2 l for drinking, 4 l for washing) in closed containers with a tap or ladle.
- **Food** that needs no cooking, for several days.
- **An emergency toilet** (a container for urine, a container with a lid for faeces, sand or sawdust – about 2 litres per person per day), screened off with a curtain.
- **Beds** for at least a third of the people, the rest sit; blankets and sleeping mats.
- **Battery lights** (no candles), a radio, spare batteries.
- **A first-aid kit**, personal medicines, respirators.
- **Rescue tools** (shovel, pickaxe, crowbar, axe, saw, hammer), a fire extinguisher, a bucket of sand.
- Containers with lids for waste and used water, hygiene products.

## Staying inside and sealing a room {#ukrytie-vnutri}

During a release of chemicals or radioactive substances, the quickest protection is to **stay in the building you are in**.

::: warning
When **heavier gases** (for example chlorine) are released, **do not shelter in the cellar** – the gas sinks. Go to upper floors. With **ammonia**, which is lighter than air, lower floors are better. Always follow the spoken message after the signal.
:::

## Life in a shelter {#ukrytie-zivot}

A stay in a shelter can last hours or days. The **shelter rules** protect everyone's health:

- Follow the instructions of the **shelter warden** and help with duties (ventilation, water, tidiness, the entrance).
- **Save water, food and light.** Keep water containers closed.
- **No smoking, open flames, candles or stoves** – in an enclosed space there is a risk of poisoning and fire.
- **No alcohol**; be considerate, keep the noise down, help older people and children.
- Keep a **daily routine**: sleep, meals, short exercise, games and activities for children.

**With radioactive fallout**, further rules apply: during the fallout (about 30 minutes after the cloud has passed) **do not ventilate**; as a rule **do not go out into the open for the first 15–20 hours**; anyone coming in from outside undresses and washes before entering (have at least 30 l of water ready); open the door only with the vents closed.

::: note
**Not allowed in public shelters:** weapons, explosives, flammables, alcohol, smelly and perishable food, bulky items (pushchairs, duvets) and, under most shelter rules, pets. If you have a pet, plan your own sheltering in advance (the cellar of your house) or ask the municipality.
:::

## The two-wall rule {#ukrytie-dve-steny}

Experience from Ukraine shows that during missile and drone attacks, when there is no time to reach a shelter, the **two-wall rule** protects you: there should be at least two walls between you and the outside. A hallway in the middle of a flat, a bathroom without a window or a staircase in the middle of the building are suitable. Keep away from windows and glass, do not sit by an outer wall, and do not stay on the top floor or in the attic. The greatest danger comes from glass splinters and debris.

## Other refuges {#ukrytie-utociska}

A refuge is not only a bunker. During long outages, heatwaves, frosts or evacuations, municipalities and states set up other places of assistance:

- **Evacuation assembly points and evacuation centres** – where evacuees gather and from where they are taken to **emergency accommodation** (schools, gyms, hostels).
- **Emergency supply points** – distribution of water from tankers, food, medicines.
- **Warming and cooling centres** – heated or air-conditioned public buildings during frosts and heatwaves.
- **Information and help points during power cuts** – modelled on Ukraine's **"invincibility points"** with heat, electricity for charging, water, internet and first aid, Sweden's **"trygghetspunkter"** and Germany's **"Notfalltreffpunkte"**.

Ask your town hall where these places are in your municipality. In the app you can mark them on the map.
