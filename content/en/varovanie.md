# Warnings, information and communication {#varovanie}

Early warning saves lives – but only if you understand it and know what to do. In a crisis, the usual channels often fail as well: the mobile network is overloaded, the internet is down and the TV is silent without power. This chapter explains how you will learn about danger, how to check information and how to stay in touch with your family.

::: urgent
**Act now – when the siren sounds**

- Go into the nearest building and close windows and doors.
- Turn on the radio or TV (public broadcaster, regional radio) and listen for instructions; also follow the municipal loudspeakers and the municipality's official accounts.
- Follow the instructions – start sheltering or evacuating only when told to, or if the danger is obvious.
- **Do not call 112 for information** – the line is for people whose lives are in danger.
- Leave children at school unless the authorities say otherwise – the school has its own plan and you would put yourself in danger.
- Alert your neighbours, especially older and deaf people.
:::

## Siren signals – example: Slovakia {#varovanie-sireny}

Every country has its own siren signals. As an example, Slovakia uses three warning signals. Each signal is followed by a **spoken message** – on the radio, TV, municipal loudspeakers or from an electronic siren – that says what the danger is and what to do.

| Signal | What it sounds like | What it means and what to do |
|---|---|---|
| **GENERAL THREAT** | 2 minutes of **wailing** (rising and falling) tone | An emergency is imminent or has happened (release of a dangerous substance, radiation accident, etc.). Go inside, turn on the radio, follow instructions. In wartime it is also used for an air raid with the announcement "Air raid alert" – go to shelter immediately. |
| **FLOOD THREAT** | 6 minutes of **steady** tone | Flooding is imminent (a dyke or dam has failed, flash flood). Leave immediately for higher ground out of reach of the water. |
| **ALL CLEAR** | 2 minutes of **steady** tone, not repeated | The danger has passed. Follow further instructions from the authorities. |

**Siren tests:** in Slovakia electromechanical sirens are tested every **second Friday of the month at 12:00** with a two-minute steady tone; electronic sirens are usually tested twice a year. The test is announced in advance in the media – if a siren sounds at any other time, it is not a test.

::: note
Signals differ between countries. In Czechia, for example, the "General warning" is a 140-second wailing tone and the test is on the first Wednesday of the month; in Germany the warning is 1 minute of wailing tone; in Austria 3 minutes of steady tone means "Warnung"; in Finland the general alarm is a 1-minute rising and falling tone. If you travel or live near a border, see the overview in the chapter [Shelters and refuges in Europe](#kryty-europa).
:::

## Other ways of warning {#varovanie-kanaly}

- **Radio and TV** – public broadcasters are obliged to transmit warnings and instructions. A battery radio is the most resilient source of information.
- **Municipal loudspeakers, police and fire vehicles with loudspeakers, door-to-door visits** – especially in smaller towns and during evacuations.
- **Mobile alerts** – in many EU countries you receive a warning directly on your phone (NL-Alert, BE-Alert, FR-Alert, IT-alert, ES-Alert, RO-Alert, AT-Alert, Cell Broadcast in Germany). In Slovakia SK-Alert is being prepared; Czechia is launching it in the second half of 2026. In your phone settings, enable "Wireless emergency alerts" / "Emergency alerts".
- **Weather warnings** – national weather services issue warnings of thunderstorms, windstorms, floods, heatwaves and frost; the Europe-wide overview is at meteoalarm.org.
- **Official websites and social media** – municipality, regional authority, interior ministry, police, fire service, public health authority.

## What to do when nothing works {#varovanie-bez-signalu}

If there is no electricity, no mobile signal and no internet:

- Turn on a **battery or wind-up radio**. Write the frequencies of the regional station and the public broadcaster in your family plan in advance.
- Go to the **agreed meeting place** at the agreed time.
- Find out whether the municipality has set up an **information point** – often at the town hall, fire station, school or community centre.
- Talk to your neighbours – share information, but check it.

## How to check information and resist disinformation {#varovanie-dezinformacie}

Rumours, false alarms and deliberate disinformation spread in a crisis. The aim may be to cause panic, divide society, undermine trust in the authorities or rob people. The Swedish guide puts it clearly: *if the country is attacked, any information that resistance is to cease is false.*

Before sharing any message:

1. **Stop.** A strong emotion (fear, anger) is a warning sign.
2. **Check the source.** Who published it? An official authority, an established media outlet, or an unknown account?
3. **Look for confirmation** in other trustworthy sources.
4. **Check the date and place** – old photos and videos are often passed off as current.
5. **If in doubt, do not share.**

::: warning
In a crisis, fraudsters offer "guaranteed" information, fake fundraisers, fake help and fake payment requests. The authorities will never ask you for passwords, PINs or payment by text message or phone.
:::

## How to call the emergency line {#varovanie-112}

**112** is the single European emergency number. It works in all EU countries, free of charge, from mobiles and landlines, and if your operator has no signal the call is connected through another network. 112 operators can also communicate in foreign languages.

When you call, say:

1. **Who** is calling and a number on which you can be called back.
2. **Where** it happened – town, street, house number, floor, landmarks; outside towns the road kilometre marker or the coordinates from your phone.
3. **What** happened – fire, accident, injury, flooding.
4. **How many** people are injured and in what condition.
5. **Do not hang up first** – wait for the operator's instructions.

::: tip
Many countries have an emergency app that sends your location automatically when you call (for example **Záchranka** in Slovakia and Czechia). Modern smartphones also send their location to 112 automatically (Advanced Mobile Location). Fill in the **Medical ID** in your phone settings; it can be displayed even on the lock screen.
:::

## Family communication in a crisis {#varovanie-spojenie}

- **Text messages** get through more often than calls when the network is overloaded. Keep them short: "OK, at home, water rising, going to grandma's."
- **A contact person outside the area** – a relative in another town or abroad. Long-distance connections are often easier than local ones, and all family members can report to that person.
- **Fixed times** – if you are saving battery, agree to switch on your phones, for example, every even hour for 10 minutes.
- **A note on the door** – if you leave, put a note on the door (inside, if that is safer) saying where you have gone.
- **PMR 446 walkie-talkies** – no licence required, range 1–5 km. Useful for the family and neighbours, for example between the house and the garden or along a street. Agree on a channel.
- **CB radio and radio amateurs** – radio amateurs help during major communication outages; if you are interested, contact your national amateur radio society.
