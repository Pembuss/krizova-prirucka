§§rozcestnik§§How to use this handbook – signpost

This handbook is not a novel to read from cover to cover. It is a tool: in calm times it helps you prepare, and in a crisis it lets you find quickly what to do. It summarises the best-rated European and international guides (Swedish, German, Finnish, Norwegian, Danish, Swiss, Czech, American, Japanese and others) together with the instructions of Slovak civil protection. An overview and rating of the sources is in the chapter Source library (→ app. B).

If a crisis is happening right now, stop reading here. Find your situation in the signpost below and go straight to the Act now box in the relevant chapter. If a life is at risk, call 112.

About national details. The handbook was written in Slovakia, so where a chapter needs a concrete example – siren signals, phone numbers, authorities, laws – it usually gives the Slovak one, often alongside Czech and other European examples. The general advice applies anywhere in Europe. For your own country, check the country overview (→ app. A) and the official websites listed there.

§§rozcestnik-zacat§§How to start if you are not yet prepared

Preparedness is not a one-off purchase but a few simple steps. Recommended order:

  1. Today (15 minutes): save the emergency numbers, find out what the sirens sound like (Warning (→ ch. 2)) and agree a meeting place with your family (Family plan (→ ch. 3)).
  2. This week: set aside water and food for 3 days (Home supplies (→ ch. 4)) and print the checklists (→ ch. 30).
  3. This month: pack grab bags (Grab bag (→ ch. 5)), find out where your shelter is (Sheltering (→ ch. 6)) and plan escape routes (Evacuation (→ ch. 7)).
  4. Within three months: extend supplies to 7–14 days (Shopping lists (→ ch. 28)), arrange light, heat and cooking without electricity (Power and essentials (→ ch. 29)) and take a first-aid course.
  5. Twice a year: check your supplies and plan using the Regular review (→ ch. 30) list.

§§principy§§Chapter 1Preparedness principles and your risks

In a major crisis, the emergency services, hospitals and authorities have to concentrate on those in the greatest danger. Everyone else must manage on their own for a while. That is why European countries ask every household to be self-sufficient for at least 72 hours. Some countries recommend more: Norway, Sweden and Estonia one week, Germany 10 days and Austria 14 days.

Act now – the basis for every crisis

  • Save in your phone and write on paper: 112 (the single European emergency number) and your country’s numbers for fire, ambulance and police (in Slovakia 150, 155, 158).
  • Find out what the siren warning signals sound like and what to do when you hear them (Warning (→ ch. 2)).
  • Agree with your family on a meeting place near home and one outside your neighbourhood (Family plan (→ ch. 3)).
  • Keep water and food for at least 3 days, medicines, a torch, a battery radio and cash at home (Supplies (→ ch. 4)).
  • Keep a grab bag ready by the door (Grab bag (→ ch. 5)).

§§principy-preco§§Why prepare

Crises come more often than we think. In recent years Central Europe has been hit by floods (for example in September 2024), extreme heatwaves, windstorms, the COVID-19 pandemic, an energy crisis, the Iberian blackout in April 2025, a large power outage in Czechia in July 2025 and the war in Ukraine close to our borders. Most crises are not a disaster for the whole country but a local event that nevertheless hits your household: a flooded street, a week without power after a storm, a village sealed off because of a chemical leak.

A prepared household:

  • protects lives – knows how to behave, where to go and what not to do,
  • reduces damage – moves belongings in time during a flood, puts out a fire while it is still small,
  • relieves the rescuers – they can focus on people in the greatest need,
  • copes with fear – a plan and supplies give a sense of control, which reduces panic,
  • helps others – neighbours, older people, family.

§§principy-urovne§§Three levels of preparedness

Level Duration What it includes
Basic 72 hours Water 3 l per person per day, food that needs no cooking, medicines, light, radio, cash, grab bag, family plan. Recommended by the EU and most countries.
Extended 7–14 days More water and food, cooking without electricity, alternative heating, an emergency toilet, power banks and a solar charger, a month’s supply of medicines. Recommended by Sweden, Norway, Germany, Austria.
Long-term 30 days or more A rotating stock of long-life food, water treatment, a garden, skills, community cooperation, a financial reserve. For economic and supply crises and long outages.

Start with the basics and build up gradually. A 3-day supply is much better than none, even if it is not perfect.

The best stock is the one you use every day. Buy food you actually eat and use the oldest first. Single-purpose “crisis kits” that sit untouched for years are more expensive and less reliable.

§§principy-fazy§§The four phases of every crisis

  1. Preparation (before the crisis) – plan, supplies, knowledge, skills, insurance. This is when you have the most time and the most influence.
  2. Warning – a siren, a message on your phone, the radio, a call from the municipality, or your own observation (rising water, smoke, a smell).
  3. Response (during the crisis) – protect life and health, decide to stay, shelter or leave, help others, follow the news.
  4. Recovery (after the crisis) – returning home, documenting damage, insurance claims, psychological support, restocking, and reviewing what to improve.

§§principy-rozhodnutia§§Three basic decisions: stay, shelter or leave

In almost every crisis you make one of three decisions. The authorities will usually tell you which one applies. If they do not, use this table:

Decision When Examples
Stay at home (normal routine using your supplies) Home is safe; the problem is a loss of services power cut, water outage, epidemic, frost, heatwave, supply crisis
Shelter (in a building, cellar, shelter) The danger is outside and short-term radiation or chemical accident, air attack, windstorm, thunderstorm, shooter nearby
Leave (evacuate) Home is no longer safe flood, fire, damaged building, landslide, ordered evacuation

If the authorities call an evacuation, leave at once. People who wait “just a little longer” during floods often become trapped or drown when they try to leave later through the water.

§§principy-rizika§§Assess your risks

Every place has different risks. Answer these questions (the app will evaluate them for you):

  • Water: Do I live near a river or stream, in a valley or below a dam? Has there been a flood here before? Do I have a cellar that could flood? Flood hazard and risk maps are published by national water authorities in every EU country (EU Floods Directive).
  • Slope and ground: Do I live below or on a slope prone to landslides, or in an earthquake zone?
  • Forest and dry vegetation: Is the house next to a forest or field where fire is a risk in dry periods?
  • Industry: Is there a chemical plant, a store of hazardous substances, or a railway or motorway carrying dangerous goods within 5–10 km?
  • Nuclear facility: Do I live within 20–30 km of a nuclear power plant? If so, find out where you would get iodine tablets (Nuclear accident (→ ch. 18)).
  • Flat or house: Do I live on an upper floor of a tower block (without power the lift will stop, and often the water too)? Do I heat with gas or electricity (both depend on electricity)? Do I have an alternative heat source?
  • People: Are there children, older people, sick people, people with disabilities, or someone who depends on an electrical device in my household?
  • Mobility: Do I have a car? Can I leave on foot? Where would I go if I had to leave home?

§§principy-omyly§§Most common misconceptions

  • “The state will take care of me.” It will, but not immediately and not everyone at once. The first hours and days are up to you.
  • “It won’t happen to me.” Most people hit by a flood or fire thought so too.
  • “Preparedness is for extremists and preppers.” Preparedness is a civic responsibility recommended by governments across Europe and by the European Union.
  • “I have to buy expensive equipment.” The basics cost little – water, long-life food, a torch, a radio and a plan.
  • “In a crisis people behave like animals.” Research shows the opposite: most people cooperate and help each other in a crisis. Panic is rare – and a plan reduces it further.

§§principy-trening§§How to learn and practise

  • Once a year, hold a family drill: for example “a weekend without electricity” or “evacuate in 10 minutes”. You will find out what is missing.
  • Take a first-aid course (Red Cross, ambulance services, driving schools) and refresh it every 2–3 years.
  • Take part in municipal exercises and find out who is responsible for civil protection in your municipality.
  • Teach children the emergency numbers, their home address and the meeting place.

§§varovanie§§Chapter 2Warnings, information and communication

Early warning saves lives – but only if you understand it and know what to do. In a crisis, the usual channels often fail as well: the mobile network is overloaded, the internet is down and the TV is silent without power. This chapter explains how you will learn about danger, how to check information and how to stay in touch with your family.

Act now – when the siren sounds

  • Go into the nearest building and close windows and doors.
  • Turn on the radio or TV (public broadcaster, regional radio) and listen for instructions; also follow the municipal loudspeakers and the municipality’s official accounts.
  • Follow the instructions – start sheltering or evacuating only when told to, or if the danger is obvious.
  • Do not call 112 for information – the line is for people whose lives are in danger.
  • Leave children at school unless the authorities say otherwise – the school has its own plan and you would put yourself in danger.
  • Alert your neighbours, especially older and deaf people.

§§varovanie-sireny§§Siren signals – example: Slovakia

Every country has its own siren signals. As an example, Slovakia uses three warning signals. Each signal is followed by a spoken message – on the radio, TV, municipal loudspeakers or from an electronic siren – that says what the danger is and what to do.

Signal What it sounds like What it means and what to do
GENERAL THREAT 2 minutes of wailing (rising and falling) tone An emergency is imminent or has happened (release of a dangerous substance, radiation accident, etc.). Go inside, turn on the radio, follow instructions. In wartime it is also used for an air raid with the announcement “Air raid alert” – go to shelter immediately.
FLOOD THREAT 6 minutes of steady tone Flooding is imminent (a dyke or dam has failed, flash flood). Leave immediately for higher ground out of reach of the water.
ALL CLEAR 2 minutes of steady tone, not repeated The danger has passed. Follow further instructions from the authorities.

Siren tests: in Slovakia electromechanical sirens are tested every second Friday of the month at 12:00 with a two-minute steady tone; electronic sirens are usually tested twice a year. The test is announced in advance in the media – if a siren sounds at any other time, it is not a test.

Signals differ between countries. In Czechia, for example, the “General warning” is a 140-second wailing tone and the test is on the first Wednesday of the month; in Germany the warning is 1 minute of wailing tone; in Austria 3 minutes of steady tone means “Warnung”; in Finland the general alarm is a 1-minute rising and falling tone. If you travel or live near a border, see the overview in the chapter Shelters and refuges in Europe (→ app. A).

§§varovanie-kanaly§§Other ways of warning

  • Radio and TV – public broadcasters are obliged to transmit warnings and instructions. A battery radio is the most resilient source of information.
  • Municipal loudspeakers, police and fire vehicles with loudspeakers, door-to-door visits – especially in smaller towns and during evacuations.
  • Mobile alerts – in many EU countries you receive a warning directly on your phone (NL-Alert, BE-Alert, FR-Alert, IT-alert, ES-Alert, RO-Alert, AT-Alert, Cell Broadcast in Germany). In Slovakia SK-Alert is being prepared; Czechia is launching it in the second half of 2026. In your phone settings, enable “Wireless emergency alerts” / “Emergency alerts”.
  • Weather warnings – national weather services issue warnings of thunderstorms, windstorms, floods, heatwaves and frost; the Europe-wide overview is at meteoalarm.org.
  • Official websites and social media – municipality, regional authority, interior ministry, police, fire service, public health authority.

§§varovanie-bez-signalu§§What to do when nothing works

If there is no electricity, no mobile signal and no internet:

  • Turn on a battery or wind-up radio. Write the frequencies of the regional station and the public broadcaster in your family plan in advance.
  • Go to the agreed meeting place at the agreed time.
  • Find out whether the municipality has set up an information point – often at the town hall, fire station, school or community centre.
  • Talk to your neighbours – share information, but check it.

§§varovanie-dezinformacie§§How to check information and resist disinformation

Rumours, false alarms and deliberate disinformation spread in a crisis. The aim may be to cause panic, divide society, undermine trust in the authorities or rob people. The Swedish guide puts it clearly: if the country is attacked, any information that resistance is to cease is false.

Before sharing any message:

  1. Stop. A strong emotion (fear, anger) is a warning sign.
  2. Check the source. Who published it? An official authority, an established media outlet, or an unknown account?
  3. Look for confirmation in other trustworthy sources.
  4. Check the date and place – old photos and videos are often passed off as current.
  5. If in doubt, do not share.

In a crisis, fraudsters offer “guaranteed” information, fake fundraisers, fake help and fake payment requests. The authorities will never ask you for passwords, PINs or payment by text message or phone.

§§varovanie-112§§How to call the emergency line

112 is the single European emergency number. It works in all EU countries, free of charge, from mobiles and landlines, and if your operator has no signal the call is connected through another network. 112 operators can also communicate in foreign languages.

When you call, say:

  1. Who is calling and a number on which you can be called back.
  2. Where it happened – town, street, house number, floor, landmarks; outside towns the road kilometre marker or the coordinates from your phone.
  3. What happened – fire, accident, injury, flooding.
  4. How many people are injured and in what condition.
  5. Do not hang up first – wait for the operator’s instructions.

Many countries have an emergency app that sends your location automatically when you call (for example Záchranka in Slovakia and Czechia). Modern smartphones also send their location to 112 automatically (Advanced Mobile Location). Fill in the Medical ID in your phone settings; it can be displayed even on the lock screen.

§§varovanie-spojenie§§Family communication in a crisis

  • Text messages get through more often than calls when the network is overloaded. Keep them short: “OK, at home, water rising, going to grandma’s.”
  • A contact person outside the area – a relative in another town or abroad. Long-distance connections are often easier than local ones, and all family members can report to that person.
  • Fixed times – if you are saving battery, agree to switch on your phones, for example, every even hour for 10 minutes.
  • A note on the door – if you leave, put a note on the door (inside, if that is safer) saying where you have gone.
  • PMR 446 walkie-talkies – no licence required, range 1–5 km. Useful for the family and neighbours, for example between the house and the garden or along a street. Agree on a channel.
  • CB radio and radio amateurs – radio amateurs help during major communication outages; if you are interested, contact your national amateur radio society.

§§zasoby-voda§§Water – the most important supply

People can survive weeks without food but only a few days without water. During a power cut the pumps in apartment buildings often stop; during a flood tap water may become unsafe to drink.

How much water:

  • Drinking and cooking: 3 litres per person per day. This is recommended by Denmark, Sweden and most other countries. Germany’s BBK works with a minimum of 2 litres (1.5 l for drinking, 0.5 l for cooking). More is needed in hot weather, during physical exertion, breastfeeding and illness.
  • Hygiene: at least 1–2 litres per person per day extra (washing hands, teeth, dishes). For flushing the toilet use bath water or rainwater, or an emergency toilet (Hygiene (→ ch. 24)).
  • Children need less (about half); formula-fed babies need water to prepare the formula. Pets: a dog needs about 50 ml per kilogram of body weight per day, a cat about 0.25 l.

§§zasoby-jedlo§§Food

§§zasoby-zasady§§Principles

  1. Long-life and no refrigeration. Tins, dried and packaged food that does not need a fridge.
  2. At least half no-cook. During a power and gas outage you must be able to eat without cooking: crispbread, tinned fish and meat, beans, sweetcorn, ready meals in tins, peanut butter, nuts, dried fruit, UHT milk, muesli, bars, chocolate. If you cook with electricity, the share of no-cook food should be even higher.
  3. What you normally eat. A crisis is no time to experiment with your stomach. Think of diets, allergies, children and pets too.
  4. Energy: about 2,000–2,200 kcal per adult per day. Children and older people need less; teenagers, pregnant and breastfeeding women need more.
  5. Low salt and low water needs. Very salty food makes you thirsty.
  6. Small packs. Once opened without a fridge, you can finish the whole pack quickly.
  7. Do not forget a tin opener (a manual one!), matches and a stove.

§§zasoby-sklad§§Where to store supplies

  • Cool, dry and dark – ideally 10–20 °C. Heat shortens shelf life.
  • Not everything in the cellar if there is a flood risk. Keep part of it higher up.
  • Spread your supplies – some at home, some in the car, at a cottage or with relatives.
  • Protect food from pests (sturdy containers) and damp.
  • Supplies for sheltering (water, food, light, blankets) can be kept directly in the cellar or the room you will use for sheltering.

§§zasoby-obmena§§Rotation: so nothing expires

The FIFO rule – “first in, first out”: put new food at the back, older food at the front, and use the older food first. That way your supply stays fresh and you throw nothing away.

  • Write the purchase date on packs and make the expiry date clearly visible.
  • A “best before” date means the food is often still safe after it if the packaging is intact. A “use by” date must be observed strictly.
  • Throw away tins with bulging, rusty or damaged lids.
  • Twice a year (for example when the clocks change), check your supplies using the regular review (→ ch. 30). The app keeps a rotation table and reminds you what is nearing its expiry date.

§§zasoby-postupne§§How to build up supplies gradually

You do not have to buy a two-week supply at once. Add a few extra items every time you shop. With a budget of €20–30 a week you will have a solid supply in two to three months. The app turns your budget into a week-by-week purchase plan, starting with the most important items (water, no-cook food, light, medicines) and moving to less urgent ones.

§§ukrytie§§Chapter 6Sheltering: shelters, basements and staying indoors

Sheltering protects you from a danger that comes from outside and is relatively short-lived: radioactive fallout, a toxic gas leak, an air attack, a windstorm, shooting. A shelter does not have to be a bunker – in most cases it is the cellar, basement or an inner room of the building you are in. What matters is knowing where to go, what to take and how to behave.

Act now – you must take shelter

  • Go to the nearest solid building – do not run a long way across open ground.
  • During an air-raid alert, go to a shelter, cellar or underground car park; if there is none, to an inner room with at least two walls between you and the outside, away from windows.
  • During a chemical or radiation release, close windows and doors, turn off ventilation, seal gaps and turn on the radio.
  • Take your shelter bag: documents, medicines, water, food, light, radio, blanket.
  • Help your neighbours and follow the instructions of the authorities and the shelter warden.
  • Go outside only after the All clear signal or when instructed.

§§ukrytie-druhy§§Types of shelter – example: Slovakia

Shelter systems differ from country to country (see Shelters in Europe (→ app. A)). As an example, Slovak legislation (Act No. 42/1994 on civil protection and Decree No. 532/2006) distinguishes these protective structures:

  • Blast-resistant shelters – protect against the blast wave, radiation, chemical and biological agents, and have filtered ventilation. Protection factor at least 100.
  • Gas-tight shelters – gas-tight, protect against radiation and dangerous substances. Protection factor at least 100.
  • Simple self-built shelters (JÚBS) – basements, cellars and other suitable spaces that are adapted only when a threat arises, according to the municipal shelter plan. Protection factor at least 50. They make up the vast majority of protective structures.

The protection factor tells you how many times weaker the radiation is in the shelter than outside. With a factor of 50 you receive only 2 % of the dose you would get in the open.

According to the Slovak interior ministry, Slovakia has about 1,530 blast-resistant and gas-tight shelters for about 4 % of the population. Everyone else would shelter in simple shelters, which must be ready to receive people within 12 hours and have improved protection within 24 hours. Blast-resistant and gas-tight shelters not used in peacetime must be ready within 2 hours; those in other use (for example as storage) within 12 hours.

§§ukrytie-kde§§How to find out where your shelter is

  1. Town hall – municipalities usually have a shelter plan listing the shelters and who should go where. Ask to see it.
  2. Regional or district civil-protection office keeps a register of shelters.
  3. The building manager or your employer knows whether the building has a shelter or a designated basement.
  4. Signs – shelters are usually marked with a sign (e.g. “ÚKRYT”, “Schutzraum”, “Schron”) and the international civil-defence emblem (a blue triangle on an orange background).
  5. The map in the app shows shelters and other places from open data (OpenStreetMap), links to official maps across Europe, and lets you add your own shelter.

Once you know where your shelter is, walk the route to it – at night, with an older person, with a pushchair. Write it into your family plan and onto your wallet card.

§§ukrytie-domov§§Prepare an improvised shelter at home

If there is no blast-resistant shelter nearby, prepare a cellar or inner room in advance, following civil-protection guidance for simple shelters.

§§ukrytie-vyber§§Choosing the room

  • Preferably below ground – the deeper, the better the protection.
  • Few windows and doors, not in a corner of the building, ideally surrounded by other rooms.
  • Close to the toilet and bathroom.
  • Allow 1–1.5 m² of floor space per person and a ceiling height of at least 2.1 m.
  • In an apartment building without a cellar: a hallway or bathroom in the middle of the flat, on a middle floor (not the top floor and not a ground floor with large windows).

§§ukrytie-utesnenie§§Reinforcing and sealing

  • Cover windows and vents at ground level with boards or sheet metal and bank them up with soil, sand, sandbags or bricks. The thickness should match the outer wall, and the material should overlap the opening by 30 cm.
  • If the entrance leads straight outside, build a blast wall in front of the door (at least 50 cm thick and 1.7 m high) that does not block the entrance.
  • Seal all gaps around windows, doors, pipe penetrations and unused vents: expanding foam, duct tape, self-adhesive foam strip, plastic sheeting, plaster, putty, clay.
  • For natural ventilation use two ducts on opposite walls – the intake draws air 1.5–2 m above the ground and opens 0.5 m above the floor; the exhaust opens under the ceiling and ends at least 1 m higher. Allow about 10 cm² of cross-section per person (for 10 people a pipe about 11–12 cm in diameter). The ducts must be closable.

§§ukrytie-vybavenie§§Equipping the shelter

  • Water: 6 litres per person per day (2 l for drinking, 4 l for washing) in closed containers with a tap or ladle.
  • Food that needs no cooking, for several days.
  • An emergency toilet (a container for urine, a container with a lid for faeces, sand or sawdust – about 2 litres per person per day), screened off with a curtain.
  • Beds for at least a third of the people, the rest sit; blankets and sleeping mats.
  • Battery lights (no candles), a radio, spare batteries.
  • A first-aid kit, personal medicines, respirators.
  • Rescue tools (shovel, pickaxe, crowbar, axe, saw, hammer), a fire extinguisher, a bucket of sand.
  • Containers with lids for waste and used water, hygiene products.

§§ukrytie-vnutri§§Staying inside and sealing a room

During a release of chemicals or radioactive substances, the quickest protection is to stay in the building you are in.

🚪 Shelter in place and seal a room

  1. Go into the nearest building and close windows and doors.
  2. Switch off ventilation, air conditioning and extractor fans.
  3. Choose a room with the fewest windows, ideally with water and a toilet.
  4. Seal gaps around windows, doors and vents with sheeting, tape and wet towels.
  5. Listen to the radio and wait for instructions; ventilate only after the all-clear.

When heavier gases (for example chlorine) are released, do not shelter in the cellar – the gas sinks. Go to upper floors. With ammonia, which is lighter than air, lower floors are better. Always follow the spoken message after the signal.

§§ukrytie-odchod§§How to go to a shelter

🏠 Taking shelter in a shelter or basement

  1. Switch off appliances, shut off gas and water, put out flames, close windows.
  2. Alert neighbours and help older and ill people.
  3. Take your shelter bag: documents, medicines, water, food for several days, light, radio, blanket.
  4. Go to the assigned or nearest shelter; if none, to a basement or interior room.
  5. In the shelter follow the shelter warden, save water and light.
  6. Leave only after the all-clear or an official instruction.

🏚️ Shelter bag

Based on Slovak civil protection advice. Do not bring weapons, alcohol, smelly or flammable substances or bulky items.

  • ☐ Documents, valuables, money
  • ☐ Personal medicines and a small first-aid kit
  • ☐ Well-packed long-life food for several days, water or tea in a flask
  • ☐ Cutlery, bowl, mug, can opener
  • ☐ Hygiene: soap, towel, toothbrush, toilet paper, pads
  • ☐ Torch, spare batteries, radio
  • ☐ Blanket or sleeping bag, warm clothing, slippers
  • ☐ Rain poncho, plastic bags (to protect hands, feet, head)
  • ☐ Book, cards, games or handicrafts to pass the time
  • ☐ Name tag on the bag

§§ukrytie-zivot§§Life in a shelter

A stay in a shelter can last hours or days. The shelter rules protect everyone’s health:

  • Follow the instructions of the shelter warden and help with duties (ventilation, water, tidiness, the entrance).
  • Save water, food and light. Keep water containers closed.
  • No smoking, open flames, candles or stoves – in an enclosed space there is a risk of poisoning and fire.
  • No alcohol; be considerate, keep the noise down, help older people and children.
  • Keep a daily routine: sleep, meals, short exercise, games and activities for children.

With radioactive fallout, further rules apply: during the fallout (about 30 minutes after the cloud has passed) do not ventilate; as a rule do not go out into the open for the first 15–20 hours; anyone coming in from outside undresses and washes before entering (have at least 30 l of water ready); open the door only with the vents closed.

Not allowed in public shelters: weapons, explosives, flammables, alcohol, smelly and perishable food, bulky items (pushchairs, duvets) and, under most shelter rules, pets. If you have a pet, plan your own sheltering in advance (the cellar of your house) or ask the municipality.

§§ukrytie-dve-steny§§The two-wall rule

Experience from Ukraine shows that during missile and drone attacks, when there is no time to reach a shelter, the two-wall rule protects you: there should be at least two walls between you and the outside. A hallway in the middle of a flat, a bathroom without a window or a staircase in the middle of the building are suitable. Keep away from windows and glass, do not sit by an outer wall, and do not stay on the top floor or in the attic. The greatest danger comes from glass splinters and debris.

§§ukrytie-utociska§§Other refuges

A refuge is not only a bunker. During long outages, heatwaves, frosts or evacuations, municipalities and states set up other places of assistance:

  • Evacuation assembly points and evacuation centres – where evacuees gather and from where they are taken to emergency accommodation (schools, gyms, hostels).
  • Emergency supply points – distribution of water from tankers, food, medicines.
  • Warming and cooling centres – heated or air-conditioned public buildings during frosts and heatwaves.
  • Information and help points during power cuts – modelled on Ukraine’s “invincibility points” with heat, electricity for charging, water, internet and first aid, Sweden’s “trygghetspunkter” and Germany’s “Notfalltreffpunkte”.

Ask your town hall where these places are in your municipality. In the app you can mark them on the map.

§§evakuacia§§Chapter 7Evacuation and escape routes

Evacuation means leaving your home or area because staying is more dangerous than going. It can be ordered by the authorities (flood, fire, chemical leak, risk of a building collapsing, war) or spontaneous, when you decide yourself that your home is not safe. In both cases: people with a plan and a grab bag leave faster and more safely.

Act now – evacuation

  1. Turn off electricity, gas and water, put out flames, close windows, lock up.
  2. Take your evacuation bag (max. 25 kg per adult), documents, medicines and pets.
  3. Leave a note on the door saying where you went (if safe to do so).
  4. Use the designated route to the assembly point or your agreed destination.
  5. Inform family and your out-of-area contact.
  6. Register at the evacuation or reception centre.

§§evakuacia-druhy§§Types of evacuation

  • Short-term – for a few hours to days (gas leak, discovery of unexploded ordnance, flood). You usually go to an evacuation centre or to relatives.
  • Long-term – when it is impossible to return for weeks or longer (destroyed house, widespread flooding, radiation accident, war).
  • Building evacuation – leaving a single building (school, office, hospital) according to its evacuation plan.
  • Area evacuation – evacuation of a whole town or region, directed by the municipality and regional authorities.

Municipalities designate evacuation assembly points where evacuees gather and register and from where they are taken to emergency accommodation. Transport is arranged for people without a car and for people with limited mobility – tell the municipality in advance if you will need it.

§§evakuacia-sam§§When to leave on your own

Leave without waiting to be told if:

  • water is rising and threatens to cut off access roads,
  • a fire is approaching the house, or there is smoke in the stairwell and you can leave safely,
  • the building is damaged (cracks after an earthquake, landslide, gas explosion),
  • you smell gas,
  • you depend on an electrical device and the outage will last longer than your backup.

Do not leave if the authorities tell you to stay inside (chemical leak, radiation, air attack). Outside you would be exposed to greater danger. If a flat below you is on fire and the stairwell is full of smoke, stay in your flat, seal the door and call 112.

§§evakuacia-ciele§§Where to go

Plan at least three destinations in advance:

  1. A nearby destination – relatives or friends in another part of town or in a neighbouring village (short-term evacuation).
  2. A distant destination – family, a cottage or friends 50–150 km away, outside the same risk (a different river basin, a different direction from an industrial plant or nuclear power station).
  3. An official place – the municipality’s evacuation assembly point or centre.

Agree with your hosts in advance so that they expect you, and decide who will let them know when you set off.

§§evakuacia-trasy§§Planning escape routes

Plan two or three different routes to each destination. The main route may be blocked, flooded or congested.

  • Primary route – the fastest under normal conditions.
  • Alternative route – a different road, ideally in a different direction or across a different bridge.
  • Route avoiding risky spots – avoids low-lying sections near rivers, underpasses, tunnels, forests (in fires) and industrial sites.

When planning, consider:

  • Rivers and bridges – in a flood they may be underwater or closed. Note which river the route crosses.
  • Underpasses and tunnels – they flood quickly in cloudbursts.
  • Wind direction – during a chemical or radiation release, move away at right angles to the wind, not downwind.
  • Fuel – range on a full tank; for an electric car, range without chargers (they do not work during a power cut).
  • Walking and cycling alternatives – the car may get stuck in a traffic jam or run out of fuel. How far can children and older people walk?
  • Petrol stations, hospitals, pharmacies along the way.
  • A paper map – phone navigation fails without signal and power. Mark the routes on a paper map and keep it in the car and the grab bag.

The app that comes with this book suggests several routes to your destinations by car, on foot and by bike, warns you about river crossings, bridges and tunnels and the proximity of nuclear power plants. You can print the routes as a card with a map and directions.

  • Keep a car kit (→ ch. 30) in the car: first-aid kit, water, food, blanket, torch, charger, paper map, cash.
  • Never drive through flowing water or flooded sections – 30 cm of moving water will carry a car away.
  • Follow the instructions of the police and fire service; do not take shortcuts through closed roads.

  • Walk on higher ground and avoid water, falling trees and power lines.
  • Keep children close, small ones in a carrier. Agree what to do if they get lost – every child carries a card with their name and a contact.
  • In traffic jams a bike is faster than a car and needs no fuel.

§§povodne§§Chapter 8Floods and flash floods

Floods are the most common natural disaster in Central Europe. They threaten not only towns on large rivers but also houses by small streams and in valleys, where water can rise within tens of minutes after a cloudburst. Most flood victims die trying to wade or drive through flowing water.

Act now – Flood and flash flood

  1. Act at once on a flood warning or a call from your municipality; do not wait for the water to arrive.
  2. If flooding is likely, switch off the main breaker and the gas valve – never while standing in water.
  3. Move people, documents, medicines and supplies to a higher floor.
  4. Take your grab bag, a charged phone and a power bank.
  5. When told to evacuate, leave immediately by the designated route, avoiding flooded sections.
  6. Never walk or drive through moving water: 15 cm can knock you over, 30 cm can carry a car away.
  7. If you are cut off, go as high as possible (not into a closed attic), signal for help and call 112.
  8. Help neighbours – especially older people and people with disabilities.

§§povodne-vediet§§What you need to know

  • A river flood follows prolonged rain or snowmelt. It is usually forecast days in advance – there is time to prepare.
  • A flash flood follows an intense storm over a small catchment. Water rises within minutes to hours, often at night and without warning.
  • Flood alert levels: most countries use three or four levels (e.g. in Slovakia and Czechia: 1 – vigilance, 2 – alert, 3 – danger). They are declared by the municipality or district authority according to the water level; at the highest level rescue work and evacuation begin.
  • A flood siren (in Slovakia “Flood threat”, a 6-minute steady tone) means immediate danger of inundation, for example when a dyke fails. Go to higher ground at once.
  • Moving water is stronger than it looks: 15 cm of flowing water can knock an adult over, 30 cm will carry a car away. Under the surface there may be open manholes, collapsed road surfaces or debris.
  • Floodwater is dirty – it contains sewage, chemicals, fuel and sharp objects.

Never enter a flooded cellar or garage where electricity may be live. Do not wade into water to save belongings, and do not drive through flooded underpasses.

§§povodne-pred§§Before a flood: prepare

  • ☐ Find out whether you live in a flood zone (flood hazard maps) and what the local flood history is.
  • ☐ Check that your home and contents insurance covers floods.
  • ☐ Prepare sandbags or flood barriers and a backflow valve for the sewer.
  • ☐ Keep documents and valuables in a waterproof bag on an upper floor.
  • ☐ Plan evacuation routes that avoid low-lying areas and bridges.
  • ☐ Keep a supply of drinking water – tap water may be contaminated after a flood.

In addition:

  • Find out whether you live in a flood zone: flood hazard and risk maps are published by national water authorities in every EU country. Ask older neighbours how high the water reached in the past.
  • Follow hydrological warnings from your national weather service and the water levels at gauging stations.
  • If possible, have electrical sockets, the boiler and the fuse box in the cellar or ground floor moved higher.
  • Do not store valuables and documents in the cellar.
  • Prepare sandbags (the municipality supplies sand), plastic sheeting, boards and silicone for sealing doors and vents, and a submersible pump that can be powered from outside the house.
  • Agree with your neighbours on help with building barriers and evacuating older people.

§§povodne-stupa§§When the water is rising

  1. Follow the radio, the municipality and the weather service. When told to evacuate, leave immediately.
  2. Move people, pets, documents, medicines, electronics and valuables to an upper floor.
  3. Move the car to higher ground outside the flood zone.
  4. Shut off the main gas and water supply and switch off the main circuit breaker. Switch off electricity only from a dry spot – if there is water near the fuse box, do not go near it.
  5. Seal ground-floor doors and windows with sandbags and sheeting; put stoppers or sandbags in toilets, sinks and drains in the cellar (sewage backflow).
  6. Move chemicals, fertilisers and fuels higher or secure them so they do not pollute the water.
  7. Get the grab bag and a supply of drinking water ready.

§§povodne-zastihne§§If the water catches you

  • Go as high as possible – to an upper floor, but not into a closed attic with no way out (if the water rises, you must be able to reach the roof or a window).
  • Make yourself visible – wave a light-coloured cloth, shine a torch at night, call 112, send a text with your exact location.
  • If you are caught in a car and it starts to move, get out through the window and climb onto the roof or to higher ground.
  • If the current sweeps you away, float on your back with your feet downstream and steer diagonally towards the bank; do not grab power lines.

§§povodne-po§§After the flood

  • ☐ Return home only when authorities allow it; have structure, electricity and gas checked by professionals.
  • ☐ Do not drink tap or well water until it is confirmed safe.
  • ☐ Photograph and document damage before cleaning up – for your insurer.
  • ☐ Wear gloves, boots and a respirator when cleaning; discard food that has touched floodwater.
  • ☐ Dry and ventilate thoroughly to prevent mould.
  • ☐ If injured, consider a tetanus booster.

In more detail:

  • Electricity – switch it on only after an electrician has checked it, if water got into the wiring or appliances.
  • Gas – if you smell gas, do not go in, do not touch switches, call the gas emergency line or 112.
  • Wells – after flooding a well must be pumped out, cleaned, disinfected with a chlorine product and the water tested. Use bottled water until the results are in.
  • Food that has come into contact with floodwater must be thrown away – including tins with damaged packaging. Wash and disinfect undamaged tins before opening them.
  • Drying out – ventilate, use dehumidifiers; have plaster and floors assessed, and remove mould wearing an FFP2 respirator.
  • Damage – document it with photos and video and report it to your insurer as soon as possible. Municipalities record damage for state assistance.
  • Health – wash your hands, treat even small wounds, consider a tetanus booster after an injury. See a doctor if you have diarrhoea or fever.

§§burky§§Chapter 9Storms, windstorms and hail

Summer thunderstorms bring lightning, cloudbursts, hail and gusts that break trees and tear off roofs. Winter windstorms can cut the power for days. Most storms are forecast by the weather service hours to days in advance – all you need to do is follow the warnings and have your home ready.

Act now – Storm, windstorm and hail

  1. Get into a solid building or a hard-topped car; do not shelter under trees.
  2. Stay away from windows and glass surfaces.
  3. Close windows and shutters; bring in or tie down loose objects on balconies and in the yard.
  4. 30/30 rule: if thunder follows lightning within 30 seconds, go indoors; wait 30 minutes after the last thunder before going out.
  5. In open terrain crouch with feet together, do not lie flat and put down metal objects.
  6. Stay at least 10 m away from fallen power lines; call 112.
  7. If driving, stop away from trees and poles and stay in the car.

§§burky-vediet§§What you need to know

  • Weather warnings in Europe use three levels: yellow (be aware), orange (be prepared) and red (take action, great danger). The same colours are used by the Europe-wide Meteoalarm system.
  • Lightning can strike 10–15 km from a storm. If you can hear thunder, you are within reach of lightning.
  • The 30/30 rule: if there are fewer than 30 seconds between the flash and the thunder (the storm is less than 10 km away), go inside immediately. Go out again only 30 minutes after the last thunder.
  • Gusts above 90 km/h break trees and damage roofs; above 120 km/h we speak of a windstorm with widespread damage.
  • Tornadoes also occur in Central Europe (for example in South Moravia in June 2021). If a funnel is approaching, go immediately to the lowest floor, into a room without windows.

§§burky-pred§§Before a storm: prepare

  • ☐ Follow weather service warnings (national service, Meteoalarm).
  • ☐ Trim dead or dangerous branches near the house; check the roof and gutters.
  • ☐ Keep power banks charged and have a torch and a battery radio.
  • ☐ Consider surge protection for sensitive electronics.
  • ☐ Park away from trees and, ideally, under cover.

In addition:

  • Keep the roof, chimney, aerial and gutters in good condition; have loose sheet metal and tiles repaired by a roofer.
  • If there is a tall tree near the house, have it assessed by an arborist.
  • Consider a lightning conductor (for a detached house) and surge protection in the fuse box.
  • Have home and contents insurance that covers windstorm and hail.

§§burky-pocas§§During a storm

At home:

  • Close windows and doors and lower external blinds (they protect glass from hail).
  • Keep away from windows; do not stay on the balcony or in the attic.
  • Unplug sensitive electronics and the aerial; during a storm do not use a landline phone or take a shower – lightning can travel through wires and pipes.
  • During hail and windstorms, go to an inner room on the lowest floor.

Outdoors:

  • Look for a solid building or a car with a metal roof (it acts as a Faraday cage).
  • Do not shelter under a solitary tree, on high ground, by a mast or in water.
  • In open terrain, crouch down, feet together, head bowed and hands on your knees. Do not lie down – you increase the area through which step voltage can pass.
  • If you are in a group, spread out several metres apart.
  • Get out of the water (swimming, boating) immediately.

In a car:

  • Stop in a safe place away from trees, poles and watercourses, switch on the hazard lights and stay in the car.
  • During a cloudburst, do not drive into flooded underpasses.

§§burky-po§§After the storm

  • ☐ Check for injuries and help neighbours.
  • ☐ Avoid damaged buildings, uprooted trees and power lines.
  • ☐ Photograph damage for your insurer.
  • ☐ Report outages to the electricity distributor, not to 112 (unless life is at risk).

In addition:

  • If someone is struck by lightning, call 112 and start resuscitation immediately if they are not breathing – a person struck by lightning is not “charged” and you can touch them safely (First aid (→ ch. 23)).
  • When removing fallen trees and branches, watch out for tension in the wood; use a chainsaw only if you are experienced and wear protective equipment.
  • Cover a damaged roof temporarily with plastic sheeting, but only if it is safe – do not climb onto the roof in the wind.

§§horucavy§§Chapter 10Heatwaves and drought

Heatwaves are the deadliest natural hazard in Europe – in summer 2022 they are estimated to have caused more than 60,000 deaths. They are most dangerous for older people, small children, the chronically ill, people living alone and those who work outdoors. Prolonged drought also brings water shortages and a risk of fires.

Act now – Heatwave and drought

  1. Stay cool during the hottest hours (11:00–17:00) and limit physical exertion.
  2. Drink regularly even if you are not thirsty; avoid alcohol and sugary drinks.
  3. Shade windows from outside during the day; ventilate at night and early morning.
  4. Cool down with lukewarm showers, wet towels and a fan.
  5. Check on older or ill neighbours and relatives every day.
  6. Never leave children or animals in a parked car.
  7. For confusion, hot dry skin or unconsciousness call 112 and cool the person aggressively.

§§horucavy-vediet§§What you need to know

  • National weather services issue high-temperature warnings in three levels, and public-health authorities publish advice during heatwaves (thresholds differ by country, typically from about 31–33 °C).
  • Cities are hotter than the countryside (the urban heat island) – attic flats and flats in tower blocks facing south or west overheat the most.
  • Some medicines (for blood pressure, diuretics, psychiatric drugs, drugs for Parkinson’s disease and others) reduce the body’s ability to cope with heat or increase water loss. Ask your doctor.

§§horucavy-pocas§§During a heatwave

  • Drink 2–3 litres a day (more during exertion), in small amounts. Older people often do not feel thirsty – they should drink according to a schedule. Urine should be pale.
  • Eat light: vegetables, fruit, cold soups, smaller portions. Take care with storing food – it spoils quickly in the heat.
  • Your home: keep windows closed during the day and shade them from outside (external blinds are up to 5 times more effective than inside curtains). Ventilate at night and early in the morning, use cross-draughts. Switch off appliances you do not need.

  • Never leave a child, an older person or an animal in a car – the temperature inside rises by more than 10 °C in 10 minutes.

  • Pets: water and shade, walks early in the morning and late in the evening; hot asphalt burns paws.

  • Observe bans on lighting fires outdoors and do not throw away cigarette ends. Dry grass and forests ignite very easily (Fires (→ ch. 12)).

§§mrazy§§Chapter 11Extreme cold and snowstorms

Severe frost, snowstorms and freezing rain disrupt transport, damage power lines and can cut off heating for long periods. The greatest risks are hypothermia, carbon monoxide poisoning from improper heating and getting stranded on the roads.

Act now – Extreme cold and snowstorm

  1. Limit travel; if you must go, tell someone your route and expected arrival.
  2. Dress in layers and protect your head, hands and feet.
  3. If the heating fails, heat one room only and seal it.
  4. Never heat with a gas stove, barbecue or generator indoors – risk of CO poisoning.
  5. If stranded in a car, stay inside, run the engine for only 10 minutes per hour and keep the exhaust clear of snow.
  6. Check on older neighbours and homeless people nearby (call 112 if at risk).

§§mrazy-vediet§§What you need to know

  • Most heating systems depend on electricity – even gas boilers, heat pumps and district heating need electricity for pumps and controls. During a winter power cut, almost every home stops being heated.
  • A well-insulated flat or house loses about 1–2 °C over several hours without heating; an older house much faster. Within a day or two the temperature can fall below 10 °C.
  • You cannot see or smell carbon monoxide (CO). It is produced by burning in an enclosed space – in a stove with a blocked chimney, from a barbecue, a gas camping stove, a generator, or a car with a snow-blocked exhaust. It causes headache, dizziness, nausea, drowsiness and death.

Never heat your home with a gas cooker, oven, barbecue or camping stove, and never run a generator in a flat, garage or enclosed balcony. People die from this every year. If you use any combustion appliance, have a CO alarm.

§§mrazy-pred§§Before winter: prepare

  • ☐ Insulate water pipes in unheated spaces.
  • ☐ Prepare a winter car kit: blanket, shovel, scraper, light, food, water, full tank.
  • ☐ Keep warm clothing, sleeping bags and vacuum flasks.

  • ☐ Sleeping bags rated below 5 °C comfort

  • ☐ Safe indoor-rated backup heater + CO alarm
  • ☐ Vacuum flasks and hot-water bottle
  • ☐ Firewood supply (if you have a stove or fireplace)

In addition:

  • Have the chimney, boiler and stoves inspected before the heating season (chimney inspections are compulsory in many countries).
  • If you have a fireplace or wood stove, keep a supply of dry firewood for several weeks.
  • Find out whether the municipality plans warming centres in case of a long outage.
  • Change the car’s wipers, tyres and antifreeze in good time.

§§mrazy-vypadok§§When the heating fails

  1. Stay in one room – ideally the smallest one, on the side sheltered from the wind, not in a corner of the house. Close the doors to the other rooms.
  2. Insulate it: hang blankets over the windows or fix plastic sheeting, put a rolled towel under the door, rugs and sleeping mats on the floor.
  3. Dress in layers: thermal underwear, wool or fleece, a hat even indoors, socks. Sleep in a sleeping bag under a duvet; keep children and older people warm first.
  4. Hot food and drinks provide energy – fill flasks with hot tea while you can still heat water.
  5. A safe heat source: a stove with a working chimney, a heater designed for indoor use (with tip-over protection and a CO alarm). Hot-water bottles and heat packs are safe.
  6. Pipes – to stop them freezing, leave a tap dripping in unheated areas, or drain the water if you are leaving.
  7. If the temperature in your home is falling below 10 °C and you have no way to warm up, move to relatives or a warming centre.

§§mrazy-vonku§§Outdoors and on the roads

  • Limit journeys during weather warnings and snowstorms. If you must travel, check road conditions and carry a winter car kit.
  • If you are stranded in your car: stay in the car (it is a shelter and you are easier to find), call 112, run the engine for only 10 minutes per hour, regularly clear snow from the exhaust, and open a window a few centimetres. Keep moving so your limbs do not get cold. Tie a brightly coloured cloth to the aerial.
  • Ice: shoes with non-slip soles or ice grips, small steps. Older people should stay at home.
  • Roofs: heavy snow can make flat roofs collapse, and snow and icicles can fall – do not walk under the eaves.

§§mrazy-zdravie§§Hypothermia and frostbite

  • Hypothermia: shivering, feeling cold, confusion, drowsiness, slow speech. Move the person somewhere warm, remove wet clothing, wrap them in blankets and a foil blanket, give warm sweet drinks (if conscious), do not massage. If they are confused or unconscious, call 112.
  • Frostbite: white or greyish, numb skin on the fingers, nose or ears. Warm gradually with body heat or lukewarm (not hot) water, do not rub, and do not thaw if there is a risk of refreezing. See a doctor.

  • ☐ Check pipes, the roof (snow load) and gutters.
  • ☐ Rewarm frostbite gradually, do not rub; seek medical care.

§§poziare§§Chapter 12Building fires and wildfires

A fire in a flat spreads within minutes. Most victims do not die from burns but from smoke poisoning – often in their sleep. Smoke alarms, a fire extinguisher and an agreed escape route are therefore among the most effective ways to protect life. With forest and wildfires, which are becoming more frequent in dry summers, leaving early is crucial.

Act now – Fire (building, wildfire)

  1. Shout "Fire!", warn others and call 112.
  2. Leave by the shortest safe route; do not use the lift or go back for belongings.
  3. In smoke, stay low and breathe through a damp cloth.
  4. Check doors with the back of your hand before opening – if hot, do not open.
  5. If you cannot get out, close the door, seal gaps with wet towels and signal from a window.
  6. If your clothes catch fire: stop, drop, cover your face and roll.
  7. In a wildfire, move away at right angles to the fire’s path, not uphill ahead of it.

§§poziare-vediet§§What you need to know

  • Smoke is deadlier than fire. It contains carbon monoxide and other toxic gases; a person can lose consciousness after a few breaths. Smoke rises – there is most air close to the floor.
  • Within 2–3 minutes a room can fill with smoke; within 5 minutes the whole room can flash over.
  • Closed doors slow a fire down and hold back smoke for several minutes. Close doors behind you as you escape, and sleep with the bedroom door closed.
  • Most common causes: unattended cooking (especially oil), candles, smoking, damaged electrical connections and extension leads, charging batteries (e-bikes, e-scooters), chimneys and heaters.

Never put water on burning oil – it causes an explosion of burning oil. Cover the pan with a lid or fire blanket, switch off the hob and let it cool.

§§poziare-pred§§Before a fire: prepare

  • ☐ Install smoke (and CO) alarms and test them monthly.
  • ☐ Keep a fire extinguisher and fire blanket in the kitchen and learn to use them.
  • ☐ Agree escape routes from every room and a meeting point outside.
  • ☐ Keep hallways and escape routes clear and door keys in a known place.
  • ☐ For homes near forests, clear dry vegetation within 10 m of the building.

In addition:

  • Put a smoke alarm in the hallway outside the bedrooms and on every floor, and a CO alarm in rooms with combustion appliances.
  • Keep a fire extinguisher (6 kg powder or foam) by the kitchen exit and a fire blanket by the cooker. Check the pressure and service date once a year.
  • Do not overload sockets or use damaged cables; charge e-bike and e-scooter batteries under supervision, not overnight in the hallway.
  • In an apartment building, do not leave pushchairs, cupboards or rubbish in corridors and stairwells – they are escape routes.
  • If you live on an upper floor, find out whether you can escape via the balcony or to the neighbours.

§§poziare-budova§§During a fire in a building

  1. Warn others: “Fire!” Wake the family, knock on the neighbours’ doors.
  2. Try to put out a small fire (bin, pan, appliance) only if it is safe – your escape route is behind you and you have an extinguisher. If you have not succeeded within 30 seconds, leave.
  3. Leave by the shortest route, closing doors behind you (do not lock them). Do not use the lift.
  4. In smoke, crawl on all fours, keep your head low, breathe through a damp cloth.
  5. Call 112 (or your national fire number) from a safe place.
  6. Meet at the agreed place outside the house and count everyone. Tell the firefighters if anyone is missing.
  7. Do not go back inside for belongings or pets – tell the firefighters.

§§poziare-uviaznuty§§If you cannot get out

  • Stay in a room with a window, close the door and seal the gaps with wet towels or clothing.
  • Call 112 and give the exact address, floor and which room you are in.
  • Signal from the window (a light-coloured cloth, a torch), but open the window only if smoke is not coming in from outside.
  • Do not jump from a height – wait for the firefighters unless there is immediate danger.

§§poziare-oblecenie§§If clothing catches fire

Stop – drop – cover your face – roll. Wrap another person in a blanket or coat (not synthetic) and roll them on the ground. Then cool the burns (First aid (→ ch. 23)).

§§poziare-priroda§§Wildfires

  • In dry periods, observe bans on lighting fires and burning grass (in many countries, including Slovakia, burning vegetation is banned all year round).
  • If you see smoke or fire in a forest or field, call 112 and give the exact location (coordinates from your phone).
  • If a fire is approaching your house and no evacuation has been ordered: close windows, doors and vents, move flammable items away from the house (wood, gas, furniture), wet the area around the house and get ready to leave.
  • When evacuating, leave in time – roads can be engulfed in smoke, and fire spreads faster than a person can run, especially uphill.
  • If the fire catches you in a car, stop in a place without vegetation, close the windows and ventilation, switch on the lights and hide under a blanket below window level. If it catches you on foot, look for a place without vegetation (a road, rocks, a harvested field, a watercourse).

§§poziare-po§§After a fire

  • ☐ Re-enter only when firefighters allow it.
  • ☐ Cool burns with lukewarm running water for 20 minutes and seek help.
  • ☐ Contact your insurer and document the damage.

In addition:

  • If you have inhaled smoke, or have a cough, headache or confusion, see a doctor – CO poisoning can show up later.
  • Throw away food, medicines and cosmetics exposed to smoke, heat or extinguishing agents.
  • Wear gloves and an FFP2 respirator when cleaning up; soot irritates the skin and airways.

§§zemetrasenie§§Chapter 13Earthquakes and landslides

Central Europe is not a region of strong earthquakes, but it is not risk-free either. Historically destructive shocks hit Komárno in Slovakia (1763), Žilina (1858) and the Dobrá Voda area in the Little Carpathians (1906), and weaker tremors occur every year. Landslides are much more common – after long periods of rain they threaten houses and roads in hilly areas. If you travel to Italy, Greece, Turkey, Croatia, Slovenia, Albania or Romania, the earthquake risk is considerably higher.

Act now – Earthquake and landslide

  1. Drop – Cover – Hold on: under a sturdy table, protecting your head and neck.
  2. Do not run outside during shaking; keep away from windows, shelves and lights.
  3. If outdoors, move to open ground away from buildings, trees and power lines.
  4. Expect aftershocks; if you smell gas, do not switch on lights, open windows and leave.
  5. Do not use lifts; leave a damaged building by the stairs.
  6. In a landslide, move away at right angles to the slide and warn others.

§§zemetrasenie-vediet§§What you need to know

  • Doorways are not safer (in modern buildings they are no stronger than the rest). It is better to get under a sturdy table.
  • Landslides are heralded by cracks in the ground, walls and asphalt, leaning trees and poles, water seeping out of the ground, cracking sounds, and doors and windows that jam.

  • Outdoors: move to an open space away from buildings, poles, trees and power lines.
  • In a car: stop away from bridges, underpasses and buildings, and stay in the car with your seatbelt on.
  • In a shop, cinema or stadium: do not rush to the exits; take cover and protect your head, then leave calmly.

§§vypadok-elektriny§§Chapter 14Power outage (blackout)

Without electricity almost everything stops: lights, the fridge, the cooker, heating, water pumps in apartment buildings, the mobile network and internet (once the backup batteries of the masts run flat), card terminals, cash machines, petrol stations, traffic lights and lifts. A short outage is an inconvenience. A widespread outage lasting more than 24 hours – a blackout – is a crisis that affects all other services. Recent examples include Spain and Portugal (April 2025) and Czechia (July 2025).

Act now – Power outage (blackout)

  1. Check how widespread it is (neighbours, battery radio) and listen to official information.
  2. Switch off sensitive appliances and leave one light on so you know when power returns.
  3. Keep the fridge and freezer closed – a fridge keeps food safe for about 4 h, a full freezer about 48 h.
  4. Save phone battery: low-power mode, texts instead of calls, short messages.
  5. In apartment blocks, draw water immediately – pumps may stop working.
  6. Use torches or headlamps; never leave candles unattended.
  7. Never use a barbecue, gas camping stove or generator indoors – risk of CO poisoning.
  8. Do not call 112 for outage information – keep it free for life-threatening emergencies.

§§vypadok-vody§§Chapter 15Water outage and contamination

Tap water can stop flowing because of a burst main, a power cut (pumps), drought, frost or a flood. It can also keep flowing but be unsafe to drink – after a flood, when the source is contaminated or when treatment fails. In that case the water company and the public-health authority issue specific instructions.

Act now – Water outage or contamination

  1. If water still flows and is not banned, fill clean containers and the bath (for non-drinking use).
  2. Follow your water company’s notice: "do not drink", "boil" and "do not use" mean different things.
  3. Use bottled water first for drinking and cooking; if told to boil, bring to a rolling boil for at least 1 minute.
  4. Ration use: drinking and cooking take priority over hygiene.
  5. Use wet wipes and hand sanitiser; flush toilets with non-drinking water or set up an emergency toilet.
  6. Find out where water tankers will be and take clean containers.

§§pandemia§§Chapter 17Epidemics and pandemics

An epidemic is an unusually rapid spread of an infectious disease in a particular area; a pandemic is its spread worldwide. COVID-19 showed that a pandemic is not only a health crisis: it also brings closed schools and borders, supply shortages, overloaded hospitals, isolation and economic problems. Experts say further pandemics (influenza, new viruses) are only a matter of time.

Act now – Epidemic and pandemic

  1. Follow public health authorities and trusted sources (national authority, ECDC, WHO).
  2. Wash hands with soap for at least 20 seconds; cough and sneeze into your elbow.
  3. Wear an FFP2 respirator in high-risk settings and limit close contact.
  4. If you have symptoms, stay home and phone your doctor.
  5. Isolate a sick household member in one room, ventilate often and separate personal items.
  6. For breathlessness, chest pain or confusion call 112.

§§jadrova§§Chapter 18Nuclear and radiological emergencies

There are more than 100 nuclear power reactors in the EU. Central Europe alone has plants at Jaslovské Bohunice and Mochovce (Slovakia), Dukovany and Temelín (Czechia), Paks (Hungary) and Krško (Slovenia), and Ukraine’s plants are not far away. A severe accident is very unlikely, but its consequences can affect a large area. A radiation emergency can also arise from an accident involving radioactive material, an attack on a nuclear facility or the use of a so-called dirty bomb.

Act now – Nuclear and radiological emergency

  1. Get inside, stay inside, stay tuned (radio, TV, municipality).
  2. Close windows and doors; switch off ventilation and air conditioning.
  3. Move to the basement or the centre of the building, away from the roof and outer walls.
  4. If you came from outside: put outer clothing in a bag, shower and wash hair with shampoo, no conditioner.
  5. Take iodine tablets only when authorities instruct, at the age-appropriate dose.
  6. Eat and drink only packaged or covered food and water.
  7. Evacuate only when and as instructed by the authorities.

§§jadrova-vediet§§What you need to know

  • In an accident a radioactive cloud may be released. It drifts with the wind, and radioactive dust (fallout) falls from it onto the ground, roofs, plants and water.
  • Exposure can be external (radiation from the cloud and fallout) and internal (inhaling or swallowing radioactive substances). Staying indoors, keeping clean and not eating contaminated food protect against both.
  • Time, distance and shielding: the less time outside, the greater the distance from the source and the more mass (walls, soil) between you and the radiation, the lower the dose. A cellar gives the best shielding.
  • Radiation from radioactive iodine and other short-lived substances falls quickly – after 7 hours to about a tenth, after two days to about a hundredth. That is why you stay sheltered for the first hours to days.
  • The emergency planning zone is the area around a nuclear power plant for which plans for warning, sheltering, iodine prophylaxis and evacuation are prepared. Its radius differs by country and plant – for example about 20 km around Mochovce and Dukovany, 21 km around Jaslovské Bohunice and 13 km around Temelín.

§§jadrova-jod§§Iodine prophylaxis

Potassium iodide tablets saturate the thyroid gland with stable iodine so that it does not absorb radioactive iodine. They do not protect against other radiation. They matter most for children, adolescents, and pregnant and breastfeeding women.

  • Residents of emergency planning zones usually receive tablets free of charge (via municipalities or the plant operator), and they are replaced regularly. Institutions (schools, hospitals, care homes) hold their own stocks. Some countries also stock tablets for the wider population.
  • Take the tablets only when the authorities tell you to (radio, TV, municipality). Taking them preventively without instruction is harmful and not recommended.
  • Dose by age (WHO recommendation; follow the package leaflet and official instructions):
Age Single dose of KI
Adults and children from 12 years 130 mg
Children 3–12 years 65 mg
Children 1 month – 3 years 32 mg
Newborns under 1 month 16 mg
  • Adults over 40 benefit less and have a higher risk of side effects; they take them only on specific instruction. If you have a thyroid condition, ask your doctor in advance.

  • If you live in a zone, ask your municipality where your iodine tablets are (do you have them at home?) and what the evacuation plan and assembly point are.

  • Keep a battery radio – instructions are broadcast on the radio during an accident.

§§jadrova-pocas§§During the accident

  1. When you hear the sirens and a spoken message about a radiation accident, go into a building immediately. If you are in a car, close the windows, switch off the ventilation and drive to the nearest building.

  3. Go to the cellar or to the middle of the building on a lower floor.
  4. §§chemicka§§Chapter 19Chemical and industrial accidents

Dangerous substances are produced, stored and transported everywhere – in chemical plants, ice rinks (ammonia), water-treatment plants (chlorine), fertiliser stores, by rail and by road. An accident or fire can release a cloud of toxic gas that drifts with the wind. Taking shelter quickly in a building is usually the best protection.

Act now – Chemical and industrial accident

  1. On a warning go indoors, close windows and doors and switch off ventilation.

  4. If outdoors, protect your airways with a damp cloth and cover your skin.
  5. If you must leave, move crosswind, away from the plume.
  6. If exposed: remove clothing, rinse skin and eyes with water for 15 minutes and call 112.

  • Do not make unnecessary phone calls; listen to the radio.

  3. Rinse your skin under running lukewarm water for at least 15 minutes, and your eyes for at least 15 minutes (remove contact lenses).
  4. If there is coughing, shortness of breath, burning eyes or unconsciousness, call 112 and say which substance was involved.
  5. Do not induce vomiting if the substance was swallowed; call the poison centre.

§§konflikt§§Chapter 20Armed conflict and air attacks

The war in Ukraine has shown that armed conflict in Europe is not a thing of the past. It is not only about fighting at the front: civilians are affected by air and missile attacks, strikes on energy and water facilities, sabotage, cyber attacks, disinformation campaigns, shortages of goods and evacuations. Membership of NATO and the EU significantly reduces the risk of a direct attack on most European countries. Yet countries such as Sweden, Finland, Poland and the Baltic states tell their citizens clearly: preparedness for war is part of defending the country.

Act now – Armed conflict and air attack

  1. On an air-raid alert go immediately to the nearest shelter or basement.
  2. If no shelter is available, go to an interior room without windows – at least two walls between you and the outside.
  3. If outdoors, lie in a ditch or behind solid cover and protect your head.
  4. Keep documents, medicines, water, a charged phone and your grab bag with you.
  5. Do not photograph or share locations of troops, strikes or shelters.
  6. Do not touch unexploded ordnance; mark the spot and report it to 112.
  7. Trust only official information – calls to surrender may be disinformation.

§§terorizmus§§Chapter 21Terrorism, violence and unrest

A terrorist attack or an active-shooter attack is rare but unfolds very quickly – seconds and minutes count before the police arrive. The proven approach from the United Kingdom and other countries can be summed up in three words: RUN – HIDE – TELL. It is just as important to know how to stop massive bleeding, the most common cause of death in attacks.

Act now – Terrorism, violence and civil unrest

  1. RUN: if there is a safe route, leave immediately and leave belongings behind.
  2. HIDE: if you cannot run, lock and barricade the door, turn off lights and silence your phone.
  3. TELL: when safe, call 112 – where, how many attackers, what they look like.
  4. When police arrive, keep your empty hands visible and avoid sudden movements.
  5. Stop severe bleeding with direct pressure, a pressure bandage or a tourniquet.
  6. Do not touch a suspicious object; move away and do not use phones or radios nearby.
  7. In unrest, avoid crowds, leave via side streets and do not stop to film.

§§terorizmus-skry§§HIDE

  • Take cover behind solid objects (walls, concrete) – plasterboard, wood or a car will not stop a bullet.
  • Stay away from doors and windows.

  • Say where you are, how many attackers there are, what they look like, what weapon they have, where they are moving, and how many people are injured.
  • When the police arrive: make no sudden movements, keep your empty hands visible (palms open), follow instructions, do not point towards the officers and do not run towards them. The police do not know who the attacker is.

  4. Wound on the torso or neck: pack a dressing or cloth into the wound (wound packing) and press.
  5. Keep the injured person warm and monitor their breathing.

  • Do not stop to film or take photos.
  • At home: close windows and doors, put the car in the garage, keep the children at home.

§§ekonomicka§§Chapter 22Economic and supply crises

An economic crisis may arrive slowly (high inflation, rising energy prices, job loss) or suddenly (payment-system outage, broken supply chains, panic buying, sanctions, war). Unlike natural disasters, it lasts not days but months or years. The best protection is a financial reserve, sensible supplies, skills and good relations with your neighbours.

Act now – Economic and supply crisis

  1. Do not panic-buy beyond your plan – hoarding worsens shortages for everyone.
  2. Use the oldest supplies first and track what is running low.
  3. Prioritise spending: food, housing, energy, medicines, transport to work.
  4. Collect prescription medicines in good time.
  5. Cooperate with neighbours – sharing and swapping save money and supplies.
  6. Beware of scams and "guaranteed" investments.

§§prva-pomoc§§Chapter 23First aid and health in a crisis

In a crisis the ambulance may be delayed or not available at all. Yet the first minutes after an injury or sudden illness are decisive. This chapter summarises the most important procedures based on the European Resuscitation Council (ERC) guidelines. It does not replace a first-aid course – hands-on practice with a manikin and bandages is irreplaceable.

Act now – basic procedure

  1. Safety – do not walk into danger (fire, electricity, water, gas, traffic).
  2. Responsive? Speak to the person and gently shake their shoulders.
  3. Breathing normally? Tilt the head back, lift the chin, look, listen and feel for no more than 10 seconds.
  4. Not breathing normally → call 112 (speakerphone) and start chest compressions at once: centre of the chest, 5–6 cm deep, 100–120 per minute. If you are trained, give 2 rescue breaths after every 30 compressions.
  5. Defibrillator (AED) – if one is nearby, have someone fetch it and follow its voice prompts.
  6. Massive bleeding – press directly on the wound, apply a pressure bandage or tourniquet.
  7. Breathing but unresponsive – recovery position on the side, and keep checking their breathing.

§§prva-pomoc-resuscitacia§§Unconsciousness and resuscitation

  • Gasping, irregular breaths are not normal breathing – start resuscitation.
  • Push hard and fast, let the chest fully recoil each time, and keep interruptions to a minimum. If you cannot or do not want to give rescue breaths, do continuous compressions.
  • Take turns every 2 minutes if there are several of you – compressions are exhausting.
  • An AED is safe and will guide you by voice. No one touches the casualty during analysis and the shock.
  • Children: if you are trained, start with 5 rescue breaths, then continue with 15 compressions and 2 breaths; if not, proceed as for an adult. For infants, compress with two fingers or both thumbs, about 4 cm deep.
  • Continue until the paramedics take over, the casualty starts breathing normally, or you are completely exhausted.

Recovery position: turn a casualty who is breathing but unresponsive onto their side, bend the upper leg, and tilt the head slightly back so the tongue does not block the airway and vomit can drain. Check breathing every minute.

§§prva-pomoc-dusenie§§Choking

  • Coughing effectively – encourage them to keep coughing and do nothing else.
  • Cannot cough, speak or breathe: 5 back blows between the shoulder blades (lean them forward), then 5 abdominal thrusts (Heimlich manoeuvre – from behind, fist above the navel, sharply inwards and upwards). Alternate until the object comes out.
  • Infant: lay the baby face down along your forearm, give 5 back blows, then turn them over and give 5 chest thrusts with two fingers. Never do abdominal thrusts on infants.
  • If they lose consciousness – call 112 and start resuscitation.

§§prva-pomoc-krvacanie§§Bleeding

  1. Direct pressure on the wound with your palm over a dressing or clean cloth. Do not let go.
  2. Pressure bandage – a sterile dressing on the wound, a pressure pad (rolled bandage) on top, and bandage firmly. If blood seeps through, add another layer; do not remove the old one.
  3. Tourniquet for life-threatening bleeding from a limb (blood spurting or flowing fast and pressure does not help): 5–8 cm above the wound, not over a joint; tighten until the bleeding stops; note the time. Improvised tourniquet: a scarf at least 4 cm wide and a stick to twist it.
  4. Wound in the neck, armpit or groin: wound packing – push a dressing or cloth deep into the wound and press for at least 3 minutes.
  5. Injured person in shock: lay them on their back, keep them warm, reassure them, nothing to drink.

§§prva-pomoc-sok§§Shock

Pale, cold, sweaty skin, a rapid weak pulse, restlessness, thirst and confusion mean the body does not have enough blood or fluid. Stop the bleeding, lay the casualty down and, if their legs are not injured, raise them; cover them (foil blanket), reassure them and call 112. Do not give food or drink.

§§prva-pomoc-popaleniny§§Burns

  • Cool with lukewarm running water for at least 20 minutes – as soon as possible. Not with ice.
  • Remove rings, watches and clothing that is not stuck to the skin.
  • Cover with a sterile dressing or cling film. Do not use ointments, oils, flour or toothpaste, and do not burst blisters.
  • Call 112 for a burn larger than the casualty’s palm, on the face, hands or genitals, for burns to the airway, electrical or chemical burns, and always for small children and older people.
  • Chemical burns: rinse with plenty of water for at least 15–20 minutes; brush off dry chemicals first.

§§prva-pomoc-zlomeniny§§Fractures, dislocations and head injuries

  • Immobilise a fracture in the position you find it; do not try to straighten it. Cover an open fracture with a sterile dressing.
  • Spinal injury (fall from a height, road accident): do not move the casualty unless they are in danger; steady the head with your hands.
  • Head injury: watch for changes in consciousness, headache, vomiting, drowsiness, unequal pupils. If any of these occur, call 112. Do not leave the casualty alone for the first 24 hours.

§§prva-pomoc-rany§§Wounds and infections when no doctor is available

  • Rinse the wound with clean (drinking or boiled) water, remove visible dirt, cover with a clean dressing and change it as needed.
  • Signs of infection: redness, swelling, warmth, pus, fever, a red streak spreading from the wound. Then see a doctor as soon as possible.
  • For deep, dirty wounds and animal bites, tetanus vaccination matters – check when you last had a booster.

§§prva-pomoc-dehydratacia§§Diarrhoea and dehydration

§§prva-pomoc-lieky§§Medicines in a crisis

  • Keep a list of medicines for each household member (name, strength, dosage, reason) and a 30-day supply.
  • Never stop suddenly taking medicines for the heart, blood pressure, epilepsy, mental illness or corticosteroids without consulting a doctor.
  • Storage temperature – most medicines up to 25 °C, some in the fridge. If refrigeration fails, check the package leaflet or ask a pharmacist.
  • In an emergency, ask a pharmacist about expired medicines; do not use expired liquid medicines, eye drops or insulin.

§§hygiena-psychika§§Chapter 24Hygiene, sanitation and mental health

In a prolonged crisis, diseases spread by dirty hands, water and waste and exhaustion from stress threaten health more than the event itself. This chapter shows how to stay clean without running water, how to set up an emergency toilet, how to handle waste – and how to look after your own mental health and that of those close to you.

Act now – hygiene without water

  • Wash your hands before eating, before preparing food, after using the toilet and after caring for a sick person – with soap and a little water, or with sanitiser.
  • If flushing or the sewer is not working, set up an emergency toilet (bucket, bags, absorbent material).
  • Put rubbish in strong closed bags outside living areas.
  • Drink only bottled, boiled or treated water.
  • Without a fridge, eat opened food as soon as possible.

§§hygiena-osobna§§Personal hygiene with minimal water

  • Hand washing: fix soap on a string to a canister or bottle with a tap and put a basin underneath. About 0.5 litres of water is enough to wash your hands. Between washes use sanitiser with at least 60 % alcohol.
  • Body: wash in sections with a flannel or wet wipes – face first, then the body, and finally intimate areas and feet. 2–3 litres of warm water are enough for a full wash.
  • Teeth: with drinking or boiled water – a glass is enough.
  • Hair: dry shampoo, or wash once every few days.
  • Menstruation: enough pads and tampons, or a menstrual cup (needs clean water for sterilising).
  • Laundry: keep plenty of underwear and socks; wash in a little water with soap and rinse several times in small amounts.
  • Dishes: disposable dishes save water; otherwise wash in one bowl and rinse in a second with a few drops of chlorine product.

§§hygiena-zachod§§Emergency toilet

If flushing does not work but the sewer is fine, you can flush the toilet with a bucket of grey water (4–6 litres) – pour it into the bowl all at once. If the sewer is not working (flood, fault), do not flush at all – the waste could back up into flats on lower floors.

How to set up an emergency toilet:

  1. Put two strong rubbish bags into the toilet bowl (emptied and with the water supply turned off) or into a bucket with a lid.
  2. Have absorbent material ready – sawdust, cat litter, peat, dry soil, paper – and a scoop.
  3. After each use, cover the faeces with a layer of absorbent (about a cupful). You can collect urine separately in a bottle or container – it smells less and the bag lasts longer.
  4. When the bag is about half full, tie it, put it in another bag and store it in a closed container outside the flat (balcony, cellar, yard) until it is collected.
  5. Wash your hands after each use.

A family of four needs roughly 10–15 litres of absorbent material and 10–20 bags per week. The app calculates the amount for your household.

§§hygiena-odpad§§Waste and cleanliness

  • Sorting: keep food waste separate in a closed container (it smells quickly and attracts insects and rodents); keep the rest dry.
  • Store rubbish outside living areas, protected from animals and rain.
  • Disinfection: clean door handles, switches, the toilet and kitchen surfaces often. Dilute chlorine products according to the instructions and never mix them with other cleaning products (a toxic gas is produced).
  • Food: transfer opened tins into a clean container; without refrigeration do not keep leftovers until the next day (especially meat, rice and dairy). Keep flies and rodents away with closed containers.

§§hygiena-psychika-zdravie§§Mental health in a crisis

A crisis is a burden on the mind. Fear, restlessness, insomnia, irritability, sadness, headaches or stomach aches, difficulty concentrating and a sense of helplessness are normal reactions to an abnormal situation. For most people they gradually fade. What helps is anything that gives a sense of control and belonging.

§§hygiena-psychika-pomaha§§What helps

  • A plan and activity – do at least one useful thing a day (check supplies, help a neighbour). Acting reduces anxiety.
  • A daily routine – getting up, meals and sleep at the same times, even in a shelter.
  • Limit the news – follow verified information two or three times a day, not continuously.
  • Contact with loved ones – talking about feelings, eating together, humour.
  • Exercise – a walk, exercises even in the flat. Breathing: breathe in slowly for 4 counts and out for 6 counts for a few minutes.
  • Help others – helping others strengthens a sense of purpose.
  • Avoid alcohol and sedatives without consulting a doctor – they worsen sleep and judgement.

§§hygiena-psychika-ppp§§Psychological first aid for others

According to WHO guidance, three steps are enough: Look – Listen – Link.

  1. Look: is the person safe? Do they have their basic needs (water, warmth, medicines)? Are they in acute danger (unconscious, suicidal thoughts)?
  2. Listen: approach calmly, introduce yourself, ask what they need. Do not force them to talk, do not judge, do not comfort with clichés (“it’ll be fine”).
  3. Link: help them get practical help, information and contact with loved ones or professionals.

§§hygiena-psychika-deti§§Children in a crisis

  • Children take their cue from adults’ reactions – try to stay calm, even if you are afraid.
  • Explain truthfully and in an age-appropriate way, briefly and clearly. Tell them what you are doing to keep them safe.
  • Expect regression (thumb-sucking, bedwetting, clinging to a parent), nightmares, anger. These are common reactions.
  • Keep up rituals (bedtime story, family meals), give them tasks (their own bag, caring for a pet) and room for play and drawing.
  • Limit their exposure to dramatic news and videos.

§§hygiena-psychika-pomoc§§When to seek professional help

If difficulties last longer than 4 weeks, get worse or stop you functioning, or if you or someone close to you has thoughts of suicide, seek help:

  • 116 123 – the harmonised European number for emotional support helplines (available in many EU countries, free of charge).
  • 116 111 – the European helpline for children and young people.
  • In Slovakia: Linka dôvery Nezábudka 0800 800 566 (24/7, free); in Czechia: Linka první psychické pomoci 116 123.
  • Your GP, a psychologist or a mental-health crisis centre.
  • If a life is in immediate danger: 112.

Some people need more help, more time and special equipment in a crisis: babies and small children, pregnant women, older people, the chronically ill, people with disabilities, people who depend on electrical devices or on medicines that need refrigeration, people living alone and people who do not understand the local language. If one of them lives with you or nearby, plan with them in advance.

Act now – when you care for a vulnerable person

  • Keep their medicines, aids and a list of diagnoses and their doctor’s contact details with them.
  • Secure power for devices (backup batteries, a portable power station) or move in good time to a place with electricity.
  • When evacuating, leave earlier – you need more time.
  • If you need help moving someone, call the municipality or 112 and say that it concerns a person with reduced mobility.
  • Do not forget the animals – lead, carrier, food, documents.

§§zranitelni-tehotne§§Pregnant women

  • Avoid exertion, overheating and getting cold; ask for priority during evacuation.
  • Know what to do if labour starts and the ambulance is delayed: call 112 and follow the operator’s instructions.

§§zranitelni-seniori§§Older people

  • Older people are at risk of overheating, hypothermia, dehydration and falls. They often do not feel thirst or cold.
  • Keep 14–30 days’ supply of medicines and a pill organiser with a schedule.
  • Glasses, hearing aid and spare batteries, dentures.
  • Regular checks: neighbours or family should get in touch every day (by phone or in person).
  • During evacuation, allow for slower movement and help with carrying luggage.
  • Dementia: a name tag with contact and address, a companion, familiar objects and routine.

§§zranitelni-postihnutie§§People with disabilities and chronic illness

  • Mobility impairment: plan step-free escape routes, find out who will help you, keep spare wheelchair batteries and a manual wheelchair as backup. In a fire or evacuation of a high-rise building, stay by the stairwell and tell the firefighters.
  • Visual impairment: a white cane and a spare, audible and tactile labelling of supplies, an agreed guide, a guide dog with its kit.
  • Hearing impairment: visual and vibrating alerts (smoke alarms with strobe), writing on paper, speech-to-text apps, informed neighbours. In most EU countries 112 can also be reached by SMS, app or relay services for deaf people – check what is available where you live.
  • Intellectual disabilities and autism: simple explanations, picture cards, familiar objects, a calm environment.
  • Chronic illness (diabetes, heart disease, epilepsy, cancer, dialysis): a supply of medicines and aids, a plan agreed with your doctor, contacts for your treatment centre.
  • Electricity-dependent devices: see Power outage (→ ch. 14). The app calculates the backup capacity you need.

§§zranitelni-zvierata§§Pets and livestock

  • Transport: a carrier for cats and small animals, a lead and muzzle for dogs. Get animals used to the carrier in advance.
  • Accommodation: some evacuation centres and public shelters do not accept animals. Make arrangements in advance with relatives, friends or an animal shelter.
  • Heat and frost: water, shade, a warm place; never leave animals in a car.
  • Livestock: during a flood or fire, move them in good time to higher or safe ground; if that is impossible, open the enclosures so they can escape. Follow the instructions of the veterinary authority.

§§sebestacnost§§Chapter 26Self-reliance: water, cooking, heat, light and power

When the grids fail, you need your own solutions for five basic needs: water, food, heat, light and power for charging. This chapter explains how to provide them safely. How much of each your household needs in different scenarios is in the chapter Power and essentials by scenario (→ ch. 29).

Act now – safety when improvising cooking and heating

  • Use combustion appliances (barbecue, gas stove, generator, gas or paraffin heater) outdoors or by an open window, and with a CO alarm.
  • Never leave open flames and candles unattended, especially around children and animals.
  • Keep a fire extinguisher or fire blanket and a bucket of water or sand to hand.
  • If several people in a room have a headache, dizziness or nausea, ventilate immediately and leave – it may be CO poisoning.

§§sebestacnost-voda§§Water treatment

When the bottled water runs out, water from a well, rain, a stream or a tanker must be treated. Follow this order:

  1. Settling and filtering – let cloudy water settle and pour it through a clean cloth, a coffee filter or a layer of sand and charcoal. This does not disinfect the water but makes the next steps more effective.
  2. Disinfection by one of these methods:
    • Boiling – the most reliable: bring the water to a rolling boil for at least 1 minute (3 minutes above 2,000 m). Let it cool in a covered container.
    • Water purification tablets (chlorine or chlorine dioxide) – exactly as instructed, usually 30 minutes to 4 hours contact time.
    • Chlorine bleach – only unscented and without additives, with the sodium hypochlorite concentration stated (usually 4–6 %): about 2 drops per litre of clear water (4 drops if cloudy), stir and leave for 30 minutes. The water should smell slightly of chlorine; if it does not, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes.
    • Water filter (ceramic, membrane, travel) – removes bacteria and parasites; only some types remove viruses. Follow the manufacturer’s specifications.
    • Solar disinfection (SODIS) – clear water in transparent PET bottles of up to 2 l in direct sunlight for 6 hours (2 days if cloudy). An emergency method.
  3. Storage – store treated water in clean closed containers and use it as soon as possible.

No home method removes chemicals, oil products or heavy metals. Do not drink water from areas affected by a chemical accident, floods with oil pollution or unknown industrial sources.

Rainwater from the roof is fine for washing and flushing; for drinking only after filtering and disinfecting (divert the first minutes of rain – they wash dirt off the roof).

How to save fuel:

  • Use a lid – it saves up to a third of the energy.
  • Soak rice, lentils and pasta in advance.
  • Haybox cooking (retained-heat cooking): bring the food to the boil, then wrap the pot in towels, blankets or a sleeping bag and let it finish cooking in its own heat for 1–3 hours.
  • Prefer food that only needs hot water poured over it (couscous, oats, instant soups, instant mashed potato).
  • Heat only as much water as you need; put the rest in a vacuum flask.

§§sebestacnost-teplo§§Heat

First keep the heat you have:

  • Stay in one room, close the doors, cover the windows with blankets or plastic sheeting, seal the gaps under the doors.
  • Layers of clothing, a hat, socks, sleeping in a sleeping bag on a mat.
  • Hot-water bottles, hot drinks, hot food, movement.
  • Sleeping together in one room (pets included) raises the temperature.

Then a safe heat source:

  • Stoves and fireplaces with an inspected chimney and a supply of dry wood.
  • Heaters designed for indoor use (for example catalytic heaters with an oxygen-depletion sensor) – only as instructed, with ventilation and a CO alarm.
  • Electric heaters only if you have a sufficient backup source (beware – they use a lot of energy: 1–2 kW).

A gas cooker, oven, barbecue, camping stove, generator or a car in a garage are not heat sources. Carbon monoxide poisoning during power cuts and frosts is one of the most common causes of death.

§§sebestacnost-svetlo§§Light

  • A head torch for every household member – hands free, safe, economical.
  • An LED lantern to light a room. Tip: a head torch strapped to a bottle of water spreads light across the whole room.
  • The same battery type (AA, AAA) for all devices; rechargeable batteries charged from a solar panel or the car.
  • Candles only as a last resort – in stable holders, away from curtains, never unattended and never in a shelter.
  • Glow sticks are safe for children and for the grab bag.

§§komunita§§Chapter 27Neighbours, community and recovery

In every major crisis, first aid comes from the people around you – neighbours, colleagues, passers-by. A well-functioning community recovers from a crisis faster. This chapter shows how to prepare together with your neighbours, how to help each other during a crisis and how to approach recovery.

Act now – helping your neighbours

  • Knock on your neighbours’ doors, especially older people and those living alone.
  • Find out who needs help (medicines, devices, mobility, children) and who can help (doctor, electrician, car, generator).
  • Share verified information – not rumours.
  • Agree on a common meeting place and time in your building or street.

§§komunita-priprava§§Neighbourhood preparedness

  • Get to know your neighbours – names, phone numbers, who has which skills and equipment, who needs help.
  • A list for the building or street (with people’s consent): contacts, needs, skills. Keep it on paper with the building manager or a trusted resident.
  • Shared equipment – pump, generator, ladder, tools, chainsaw, trailer. Not everyone needs to own everything.
  • Joint exercises – for example switching off the building’s power for an evening, or evacuating the building.
  • Contact with the municipality – who is responsible for crisis management, where the information points will be.
  • Volunteering – volunteer fire brigades, the Red Cross, mountain rescue, municipal civil-protection units, charities. Organised volunteering is more effective in a crisis than individual help.

§§komunita-pocas§§During a crisis

  • Check on each other at agreed times.
  • Divide tasks: who fetches water, who gets information from the town hall, who looks after the children, who helps older people.
  • Share: cooking for several households saves fuel, and one generator can charge the phones of a whole street.
  • Security: if thefts occur in the area, do not let strangers into the building and report suspicious behaviour to the police.
  • Do not forget yourselves – helpers need rest too.

§§komunita-obnova§§After the crisis: recovery

§§komunita-navrat§§Returning home

  1. Return only when the authorities allow it.
  2. Check the structure for cracks and sunken floors – if in doubt, wait for an expert.
  3. Turn electricity and gas back on only after a professional has checked them, if they were affected by water or fire.
  4. Water – drink bottled water until the water company or health authority confirms the tap water is safe.
  5. Food – check it after a refrigeration failure, flood or fire, and throw it away if in doubt.

§§komunita-poistenie§§Documentation and insurance

  • Photograph and film the damage before cleaning up – whole rooms and details, the rating plates of appliances.
  • Make a list of damaged items with approximate value and purchase date; keep receipts and invoices for repairs.
  • Report the claim to your insurer as soon as possible and arrange an inspection. Do not throw away damaged items until the loss adjuster has seen them (unless hygiene makes it unavoidable).
  • Municipalities record damage for state assistance and coordinate humanitarian aid – ask at the town hall.

§§komunita-upratovanie§§Clean-up and health

  • Gloves, wellington boots, an FFP2 respirator, safety glasses.
  • Watch out for mould after a flood, asbestos in old buildings after a fire or windstorm, sharp objects and power lines.
  • Drink enough and take breaks – physical exhaustion causes injuries.

§§komunita-psychika§§Psychological recovery

Losing your home, possessions or loved ones leaves its mark. Talk about your experiences, keep a routine, and be patient with yourself and your children. If difficulties persist, seek help (Mental health (→ ch. 24)). Memorial and community gatherings help the whole community.

§§komunita-vyhodnotenie§§Review and improve

After the crisis, sit down with your family and answer:

  • What worked? What was missing? What would we do differently?
  • Which supplies did we use and need to replace?
  • How should we update the family plan, grab bags and contacts?

Replenish your supplies and update your plan – the next crisis will find you better prepared.

§§energie-scenare-vypocet§§How we calculate energy

  • Phone: an ordinary smartphone battery holds 15–20 Wh. In power-saving mode one charge lasts 1–2 days. We assume 9 Wh per phone per day.
  • A 20,000 mAh power bank holds about 74 Wh, of which about 48 Wh is usable after losses – roughly 3 phone charges.
  • A head torch with 3 AAA batteries runs about 30 hours on medium. With 3 hours of light a day, a person uses about 0.3 AAA batteries a day.
  • A radio with 2 AA batteries plays for about 50 hours; an LED lantern with 3 AA batteries lights for about 40 hours.
  • A 227 g cartridge in a camping stove lasts about 2 hours on a medium flame. A household needs about 15 minutes a day for cooking and heating water plus another 8 minutes per person.
  • A fridge uses 0.5–1.5 kWh a day, a freezer about the same. An electric heater uses 1–2 kW per hour – unrealistic on batteries.
  • Medical devices: CPAP about 30–60 W (300–500 Wh per night), an oxygen concentrator 150–400 W continuously (tens of kWh a day – a generator or oxygen cylinders are needed).

§§scenare§§Chapter 32Story scenarios: you decide

Knowing what to do and doing it under pressure are two different things. In these six stories you find yourself in a crisis and make a decision at every step. Some choices move you safely forward; others have consequences. Every risky decision comes with a tip and a link to the chapter where you can learn more. The stories are fictional, but the situations and mistakes in them happen again and again in real crises.

§§scenare-ako§§How to play

  • On the website and in the app, press ▶ Play the story next to a story. The choices appear one by one, and at the end you see your result, tips to review and a list of things worth preparing.
  • On paper and in the PDF, each story is divided into numbered sections (§). Start at the section given for the story and turn to the next number according to your choice. The sections are deliberately shuffled so you cannot see ahead how a choice turns out.
  • A section marked ✔ means a good decision; ✖ means a risky one. At the end, count the ✔ marks on your path.

Play together with your family or children – everyone votes on what they would do, then you read the outcome together. It is a quick, low-pressure way to rehearse your family plan.

§§kryty-europa§§Appendix AShelters and refuges in Europe

European countries protect their populations in very different ways. Switzerland and Finland have built shelters for decades and have room for most of their inhabitants. Sweden, Norway and Denmark have extensive shelter networks and are renewing them. Central and Western Europe mostly retain Cold War shelters in varying condition, and many countries rely on sheltering in buildings, cellars, underground car parks and metro stations. Since 2022 most countries have been reviewing their systems: Poland has introduced compulsory “temporary shelter places” in new buildings, Germany is mapping “public refuges” and Norway has reinstated the obligation to build shelters.

There is no single European shelter database. This chapter therefore gives an overview of the system in each country with links to official maps and information. The app and the website also show shelters, bunkers, assembly points and other important places from OpenStreetMap open data anywhere in Europe.

The information is current as of October 2026 and changes. Before you travel, or in a crisis, check it on the official websites listed for each country. Shelters from open data (OpenStreetMap) are incomplete, and their condition and accessibility have not been verified.

§§kryty-europa-hladanie§§How to find a refuge in another country

  1. Listen to local instructions – radio, mobile alerts (EU-Alert), announcements, hotel staff.
  2. Look at the country’s official map (links below) or the local civil-protection app.
  3. Look for signs – the international civil-defence emblem (a blue equilateral triangle on an orange background) and words such as Schutzraum, Skyddsrum, Väestönsuoja, Tilfluktsrom, Schron, Úkryt, Óvóhely, Sklonište, Adăpost, Укриття.
  4. If you find nothing – the nearest solid building, cellar, underground car park or metro station; otherwise the two-wall rule (Sheltering (→ ch. 6)).

§§zdroje-zaver§§What follows from this

  • The best-rated guides (Swedish, Japanese, German, Czech, Norwegian) share brevity, a clear “before – during – after” structure, checklists and availability in several languages and formats.
  • The recommended period of self-sufficiency in Europe ranges from 72 hours (EU, Czechia, Finland, France, the Netherlands) to 7 days (Sweden, Norway, Switzerland, Estonia), 10 days (Germany) and 14 days (Austria). This handbook recommends starting with 72 hours and gradually moving to 7–14 days.
  • Water – most sources recommend 3 litres per person per day for drinking and cooking; Germany’s BBK 2 litres. The app lets you choose the standard.
  • The EU Preparedness Union Strategy (2025) asks all member states to promote at least 72 hours of self-sufficiency; more and more countries are publishing household guides. Slovak official materials are technically sound (especially on sheltering and warning) but scattered; a single modern household guide and a public shelter map are still missing – this handbook tries to fill that gap.

§§slovnik-pojmy§§Glossary

AED (automated external defibrillator) – a device that analyses the heart rhythm in cardiac arrest and delivers an electric shock if needed. It gives voice instructions and can be used by anyone.

Blackout – a widespread, long-lasting interruption of the electricity supply over a large area.

Civil protection – the set of measures to protect life, health and property in emergencies: warning, sheltering, evacuation, individual protection, emergency supplies and accommodation.

Decontamination – removing dangerous (radioactive, chemical, biological) substances from people, clothing, objects and surfaces.

Evacuation – moving people, animals and property out of an endangered area.

Evacuation assembly point – a place designated by the municipality where evacuees gather before being taken to emergency accommodation.

EU-Alert / Cell Broadcast – a warning system that sends a message to every phone in the affected area without any registration.

FFP2 – a respirator that filters at least 94 % of particles; it protects against aerosols, dust and smoke particles (not gases).

Integrated rescue system – the cooperation of the fire service, ambulance service, police, mountain rescue and other services, coordinated through the 112 line.

Isolation and quarantine – isolation separates sick people; quarantine separates healthy people who may have been exposed to an infection.

Iodine prophylaxis – taking potassium iodide tablets to protect the thyroid from radioactive iodine; only on official instruction.

JÚBS – the Slovak term for a simple self-built shelter: a cellar or basement adapted for sheltering according to the municipal shelter plan.

Emergency situation – a state declared by a municipality or regional authority when life, health, property or the environment are threatened; it allows measures (evacuation, restrictions) to be ordered.

Emergency event – a natural disaster, accident, catastrophe, public-health threat or terrorist attack.

State of emergency, state of exception, state of war – crisis states declared by the government, parliament or president; they allow some rights to be restricted and duties to be imposed.

Protection factor (PF) – the number that indicates how many times weaker radiation is inside a shelter than in the open.

Carbon monoxide (CO) – a colourless, odourless gas produced by incomplete combustion. It is lethally poisonous and can only be detected with a CO alarm.

Shelter plan – a municipal document that designates shelters and assigns residents to them.

Power bank – a portable battery for charging phones and other devices.

Radioactive fallout – radioactive dust that falls from a cloud to the ground after a nuclear accident or explosion.

Stock rotation (FIFO) – using the oldest supplies first and continuously replacing them.

Flood alert levels – in Slovakia and Czechia: 1 – vigilance, 2 – alert, 3 – danger.

Tourniquet – a device for stopping life-threatening bleeding from a limb.

Shelter – a structure or space that protects the people in it from the effects of emergencies and weapons.

Warning signal – a siren sound that warns the population of imminent or actual danger; in Slovakia “General threat”, “Flood threat” and “All clear”, in Czechia “General warning”.

Emergency planning zone – the area around a nuclear facility for which population-protection plans have been prepared (in Slovakia 20–21 km).
